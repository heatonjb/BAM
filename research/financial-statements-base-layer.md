# Financial Statements as the Base Layer of the Business Model

For a business to be tractable as an agentic harness — a graph of stocks and flows that a strategy agent can query, simulate, and optimise — the foundational representation is the triple of financial statements. This document shows how each statement and each line item decomposes into a driver graph, which drivers are causally upstream (operational levers) versus downstream (accounting artefacts), and concludes with a concrete node-and-edge decomposition of a generic P&L and balance sheet.

## The Accounting Identity as the Root Node

The entire financial representation hangs from one root constraint: assets equal liabilities plus equity, `A = L + E`, which must hold in every period and, in a forecast model, must keep holding under every changed assumption [T2] Financial Edge Training, "Balancing the Balance Sheet," §Data Capture, 2020. A second structural fact anchors the temporal semantics of every node: the income statement reports flow over a period while the balance sheet is a stock at a point in time, which is why ratios mixing the two (e.g. asset turnover) are conventionally computed on average balance sheet values [T2] CFA Institute, "How Much Does Apple Make? A DuPont Analysis," §Start with return on equity, 2013.

Retained earnings is the hinge between statements: net income flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet, and the cash balance on the balance sheet is the terminal node of the cash-flow statement [T2] Wall Street Prep, "How are the Three Financial Statements Linked?" §How are the Income Statement and Balance Sheet Linked?, 2026. Everything downstream is recomputable from a small number of upstream drivers plus these identity constraints — which is precisely what makes the three-statement model a graph rather than three tables.

Ontologically, the statements are an aggregated, double-entry view of economic events. The REA (Resources–Events–Agents) accounting model, introduced by McCarthy in *The Accounting Review*, models a firm as economic resources linked to economic events in which agents participate, connected by stock-flow relationships (events increase or decrease a resource stock) and duality relationships (a "give" event is paired with a "take" event in an exchange) [T1] McCarthy, "The REA Accounting Model," *The Accounting Review*, 1982, §Stock-flow and duality relationships. REA's design insight is that debit–credit judgments need not be embedded in the transaction record; they are deferred to database views derived from event-level data [T2] Michigan State University Broad College, "McCarthy named to Accounting Hall of Fame," 2026. The implication for a business-as-machine model is direct: build the model on events (sales, purchases, payments, hiring) and the stocks they move, and derive the P&L, balance sheet and cash-flow statement as projection views — the architecture REA formalises and which now underpins enterprise ERP design [T1] Laurier, Kiehn & Polovina, "REA2: A unified formalisation of the Resource-Event-Agent ontology," *Applied Ontology*, 2018, §Resource-Event-Agent ontology.

## DuPont Decomposition: From ROE to a Tree of Ratios

The classic starting decomposition is the DuPont analysis, named for the chemical company that began using it in the 1920s. It decomposes return on equity through a mathematical identity into three factors:

```
ROE = Net income / Equity
    = (Net income / Sales) × (Sales / Assets) × (Assets / Equity)
    = Profit margin × Asset turnover × Financial leverage
```

Applied to Apple's fiscal 2012: ROE of 42.84% = 26.66% net margin × 107.04% asset turnover × 150.10% leverage [T2] CFA Institute, "How Much Does Apple Make? A DuPont Analysis," §Decomposing Return on Equity, 2013. The three steps ask three different questions — how profitably the business runs, how efficiently it uses assets, how much debt it relies on — and each is a lever a strategy agent could pull [T2] CFA Institute, §Extracting Information from the Component Pieces, 2013.

The extended 5-step version continues the chain through the tax and interest layers:

```
ROE = (Net income/EBT) × (EBT/EBIT) × (EBIT/Sales) × (Sales/Assets) × (Assets/Equity)
    = Tax burden × Interest burden × Sales margin × Asset turnover × Financial leverage
```

For Apple this is 74.84% × 100% × 35.63% × 107.04% × 150.10% = 42.84% [T2] CFA Institute, §The Extended DuPont Analysis, 2013. Two of those five factors — tax burden and interest burden — are downstream results of decisions made elsewhere in the model (capital structure and tax policy) rather than operational levers in their own right, an early instance of the lever-versus-artefact distinction developed below.

Academic work refined the framework into an operating-versus-financing split that is materially better suited to a driver graph. Nissim and Penman (2001) restructure the DuPont chain around return on net operating assets (RNOA), separating operating from financing activities, and introduce operating liability leverage (OLLEV) — the extent to which operating liabilities such as payables reduce net operating assets and thereby lever up operating profitability [T1] Nissim & Penman, "Ratio Analysis and Equity Valuation: From Research to Practice," *Review of Accounting Studies*, 2001, §Extended profitability analysis. This cleanly separates the operating subgraph (driven by customers, products, pricing, capacity) from the financing subgraph (driven by capital-structure policy), with only interest leakage between them. Soliman (2007) shows empirically that the DuPont components carry information beyond ROE itself: changes in asset turnover and profit margin help predict future profitability and stock returns, which is the empirical warrant for treating the components as real drivers rather than algebraic tautologies [T1] Soliman, "The Use of DuPont Analysis by Market Participants," *The Accounting Review*, 2007, as characterised in: [T2] "DuPont analysis, future firm performance, and stock returns," ZHAW working paper, §DuPont analysis. 

The known failure mode is instructive for graph-building: with negative equity, DuPont's leverage factor flips sign and the decomposition becomes meaningless, and cross-industry comparisons mislead because the same ROE can arise from pricing power in one firm and volume-plus-leverage in another [T3] Ryan O'Connell, "DuPont Analysis Explained: The 3-Factor ROE Formula," https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/video/dupont-analysis-explained-the-3-factor-roe-formula. A robust harness must therefore guard constraints (equity > 0, denominator validity) as model invariants.

## Driver-Based Planning and the Integrated Three-Statement Model

The operational counterpart of DuPont-style decomposition is driver-based planning: expressing each line item as a function of a small hierarchy of causal, measurable drivers rather than extrapolating the historic line itself. KPMG defines it as a framework that plans business performance from a hierarchy of company-specific value drivers derived from strategic objectives, connected to financial and operational levers at cascading levels through "value driver trees" [T2] KPMG, "Driver-based planning," §What is driver-based planning?, 2024. Driver trees deliberately combine internal drivers (FTE counts, average salaries, volumes) with external ones (labour-market conditions, inflation) and are embedded in EPM systems so that changes in assumptions cascade automatically [T2] KPMG, §What is the role of technology in the transition to driver-based planning?, 2024.

The pattern is canonically instantiated in planning software. Oracle's driver-based Financials documentation ships a reference taxonomy of out-of-the-box drivers: revenue from volume, average selling price, discount percentage, return percentage, service hours and service rate; cost of sales from material unit cost, COGS%, overhead%, freight%; expense lines driven by headcount, attrition rate and average salary; and — critically — balance-sheet lines driven by DSO (accounts receivable), days in inventory, and prepaid/accrued percentages; cash flow is not modelled directly but derived from the timing of the revenue and expense drivers [T2] Oracle Cloud EPM, "Driver-Based Planning in Financials," §Out-of-the-box drivers in Financials and §Balance sheet drivers, F97847-01, July 2024. In Oracle's balance-sheet planning flow, retained earnings is derived automatically and cash flow is calculated indirectly from changes in non-cash balance-sheet items — the same logic as a three-statement model [T2] Oracle Cloud EPM, "Performing balance sheet and cash flow planning in Financials," §Background.

The canonical three-statement model makes the causal edges explicit and complete:

1. Net income flows from the income statement into retained earnings on the balance sheet, and is the seed of the cash-flow statement.
2. Non-cash items (depreciation, amortisation) are added back to net income; these come from the PP&E schedule.
3. Changes in working-capital accounts (receivables, inventory, payables) are recorded on the cash-flow statement.
4. Capital expenditure increases PP&E on the balance sheet; depreciation reduces it; both appear on the cash-flow statement under investing and operating respectively.
5. The debt schedule feeds interest expense to the income statement, debt balances to the balance sheet, and repayments/drawdowns to financing cash flow [T2] A Simple Model, "Three Statement Model Links," §Debt Schedule and §PP&E Schedule, 2022.

The modelling convention that makes this a closed graph is that cash is the *plug*: the analyst forecasts every balance-sheet account except cash, then constructs the cash-flow statement from the period-to-period changes of all non-cash accounts, and the closing cash/revolver balance is whatever reconciles the balance sheet [T2] Financial Edge Training, "Balancing the Balance Sheet," §How to Balance the Balance Sheet, 2020. A negative plug balance is represented as a revolver (short-term borrowing), computed with `MAX/MIN` logic and looped back into interest expense, creating the one circular reference in the model [T2] Financial Edge Training, "3-Statement Model," §Build the cash flow statement using the rules of cash, 2024. Foundationally, three links carry almost the entire model: net income into retained earnings, closing cash into the cash balance, and receivables/payables into the working-capital lines of the cash-flow statement [T2] CFI, "What is a 3-Statement Model?", §How Do the Three Statements Connect?, 2026.

Good practice on which accounts should be drivers is itself an epistemic filter: some accounts should be trend-based, some direct-entry, and some driver-based; the right number of drivers is "the smallest number necessary to explain/forecast the outcome and drive an action" [T2] AFP, "FP&A Guide to Driver-based Models and Plans," §Driver-based models and plans, 2024.

## Unit Economics: Contribution Margin, CAC, LTV, Payback

Between the top-line aggregate (revenue) and the bottom-line aggregate (net income) sits the layer that operationalises the business model: unit economics. The atomic quantity is contribution margin — selling price per unit minus variable cost per unit — which is the per-unit contribution toward covering fixed costs and the slope of the profit line [T2] OpenStax, "Principles of Accounting, Vol. 2," §3.1 Contribution Margin, 2019; [T2] Wikipedia, "Contribution margin," §Unit Contribution Margin. Contribution margin differs from gross margin in a way that matters for driver choice: gross margin is determined by accounting standards (deducting allocated fixed manufacturing costs), whereas contribution margin separates costs on an economic basis of fixed versus variable [T2] Wikipedia, §Contribution margin vs. gross margin. Break-even analysis then emerges trivially: break-even volume equals fixed costs divided by contribution per unit [T2] Yale School of Management, "A Primer on Breakeven Analysis," §Figure 6, 2024.

For recurring-revenue businesses the unit-level graph is conventionally:

```
LTV = (ARPA × Gross margin %) / Monthly churn rate
CAC payback (months) = CAC / (ARPA × Gross margin %)
LTV:CAC = LTV / CAC
```

LTV must be computed on gross margin, not revenue, because revenue-based LTV ignores the cash available to cover acquisition and fixed spend [T3] Fiscallion, "SaaS unit economics: CAC, LTV, payback and the Rule of 40," 2026-04-27, https://www.fiscallion.io/blog/saas-unit-economics. Churn is the dominant lever: halving churn from 4% to 2% monthly doubles LTV and halves the payback period, precisely because churn sits in the denominator [T3] Calcrux, "Unit Economics Calculator," 2026-06-02, https://calcrux.com/tools/startup/startup-unit-economics-calculator. Industry central tendencies are well documented: median LTV:CAC around 3:1–3.5:1 across SaaS datasets, gross margins at 70–80% for healthy SaaS, top-quartile CAC payback near 12–16 months versus 47 months for the bottom quartile [T3] CloudZero, "The Complete SaaS Unit Economics Guide," §Benchmarks, 2026-04-20, https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/saas-unit-economics.

The two headline ratios answer different causal questions and must be tracked together: LTV:CAC is a long-horizon, eventual-return metric, while CAC payback is a short-horizon cash-timing metric; a business can be "good" on the first and "bad" on the second, and payback is what actually constrains runway [T3] Inflection CFO, "Why Blended Metrics," 2026-07-30, https://inflectioncfo.co/blog/saas-unit-economics-the-blended-metric-trap; [T2] The SaaS CFO, "SaaS Showdown: CAC Payback vs LTV/CAC," 2018-07-05, §Four Scenarios. Practitioner refinements are directly relevant to harness design: CAC must allocate only *new*-customer sales and marketing spend (a mature company may split 70/30 between existing and new) or the unit economics are understated [T2] The SaaS CFO, "How to Calculate Your Overall CAC Payback Period," 2022-03-09, §What is CAC. Aggregate ("blended") metrics hide cohort divergence — a blended 3.2:1 ratio can conceal a 1.8:1 latest cohort — so the graph should carry cohorts as first-class nodes [T3] Inflection CFO, §Track CAC Payback Period, https://inflectioncfo.co/blog/saas-unit-economics-the-blended-metric-trap.

Sitting upstream of CAC are the tapering funnel drivers (visitors → leads → opportunities → closed deals), such that revenue is a product of principally operational quantities: price, volume, mix, renewal rates, and expansion rates [T3] KPI Tree, "Metric Decomposition," 2024-12-22, https://kpitree.co/guides/core-concepts/metric-decomposition. The price–volume–mix decomposition formalises this: any revenue change splits into a price effect (same volume, different price), a volume effect (same price, different quantity), and a mix effect (different composition of products/segments), each warranting a different management response [T3] eFutures CFO, "Variance Analysis and the Art of the Narrative," 2026-03-21, §Revenue Variance Decomposition. Firms publish exactly such revenue bridges in practice, e.g. a "price / volume-and-mix / FX / divestiture" walk of year-on-year revenue [T2] JELD-WEN, "Fourth Quarter & Full Year 2025 Results," §Revenue Bridge, 2026.

## Activity-Based Costing: Decomposing Costs into the Activity Hierarchy

On the cost side, the analog of unit economics is activity-based costing (ABC), which replaces volume-based overhead allocation with multiple cost drivers grouped into activity cost pools [T2] OpenStax, "6.3 Calculate Activity-Based Product Costs," §Establishing an Activity-Based Costing System, 2019. The canonical five steps — identify activities, determine activity cost pools, calculate activity rates per pool, allocate to cost objects, compute unit costs — map one-to-one onto graph construction: activities are nodes, drivers are the edge weights, and cost objects are the sink nodes [T2] OpenStax, §Five stages of the ABC process, 2019.

The key structural insight is the *cost hierarchy*, attributed to Cooper (1990) and popularised by Cooper and Kaplan (1991), which classifies activities by the level at which they consume resources:

- **Facility-level** activities (plant rent, facility management) sustain operations and do not vary with units, batches or products;
- **Product/customer-level** activities (product development, product advertising) support specific products and vary over a shorter horizon than facility costs;
- **Batch-level** activities (machine setups, quality inspections) vary with the number of batches, not the number of units per batch;
- **Unit-level** activities (direct materials, direct labour, machine energy) vary with each unit produced [T2] LibreTexts, "Managerial Accounting," §3.8 The Hierarchy of Costs, 2023 (after [T1] Cooper, "Cost classification in unit-based and activity-based manufacturing cost systems," *Journal of Cost Management*, 1990).

Empirical work confirms the hierarchy matters: batch- and product-variety-related activities are significantly associated with resource consumption, and crude measures of activity promote distorted cost assignment [T1] "Evidence on the Cost Hierarchy," *Journal of Management Accounting Research*, 25(1), 2013. Critically for modelling, ABC's cost objects need not be products: the same driver machinery costs customers, customer segments, distribution channels and sales territories, which links the cost subgraph directly to unit economics [T2] Burch, "Chapter 10: The Activity-based Costing System," §First-Stage and Second-Stage Activity Drivers, 2006. ABC is a *managerial* (internal) lens: allocating selling and G&A costs to products is not GAAP-permissible for external reporting but is entirely appropriate — indeed necessary — for internal decision-making [T2] LibreTexts, §External Reporting and Internal Decision Making, 2023.

## Throughput, Working Capital, and the Constraint Layer

Two further lenses add dynamics that a static P&L misses: throughput accounting and the cash conversion cycle.

Throughput accounting (TA), grounded in Goldratt's Theory of Constraints, measures a business with three stocks/flows — **throughput** (the rate at which the system generates money through sales, i.e. revenue minus totally variable cost), **inventory/investment** (all money invested in things it intends to sell), and **operating expense** (all money spent turning inventory into throughput) — and refuses to allocate fixed costs to units [T2] SAGE Encyclopedia of Healthcare Management, "Throughput Accounting (TA)," 2007; [T2] Goldratt Marketing, "Learning the Basis of Throughput Accounting," §Defining T, I, OE. TA subtracts only *totally variable* costs from revenue, treating everything else as operating expense, so it is more conservative than contribution margin in what it hides in overhead [T2] SAGE, §Throughput Accounting: Basic Assumption and Definitions, 2007. Its operational claim is that cost accounting actively misleads: in high-demand-variation scenarios traditional methods can overstate net income by up to 70% relative to throughput accounting, and the classic P&Q example flips a reported $300 deficit into a $300 profit [T1] Costas et al., "Applying Goldratt's Theory of Constraints to reduce the Bullwhip Effect," *Expert Systems with Applications*, 2015; [T1] Dugdale & Colwyn Jones, "Throughput Accounting: Transforming Practices?", *British Accounting Review*, 1998. For a driver graph, TA contributes the constraint variable: capacity and bottleneck nodes, with the instruction to subordinate everything else to the system constraint [T2] MIT CTL, "Lecture: Working Capital / APS," §Measuring Operations Performance, 2016.

The cash conversion cycle is the time-structure of the working-capital subgraph:

```
CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO
```

where DIO (days inventory outstanding) is driven by COGS and inventory turns, DSO by revenue and collection terms, and DPO by COGS and supplier terms [T2] J.P. Morgan, "Understanding & Optimizing Your Cash Conversion Cycle," §How to calculate the CCC, 2025; [T2] MIT CTL, §CCC: Cash Conversion Cycle, 2016. The three drivers are individually actionable levers — inventory policy, credit terms, supplier payment terms — and each directly changes the balance-sheet stocks of inventory, receivables and payables, hence operating cash flow. Empirically, the average S&P 1500 company ran a 61–68 day CCC between 2011 and 2018, with large firms converting about three days faster on collections and taking about 13 more days to pay suppliers [T2] J.P. Morgan, "Working Capital Index," §Key Findings, 2019. Geoeconomic shocks have since lengthened the cycle — global CCC rose again in 2025 to roughly 63–70 days depending on region, with inventory the dominant driver as firms hold strategic buffer stock for security-of-supply reasons [T2] Allianz Trade, "Cash at risk: Inventories are driving a new cycle of working capital strain," §Diverging trends, uneven levels, 2026-07-16. The CCC can even go negative — Dell in the 1990s collected cash from customers before paying suppliers, making the business self-financing at scale, a structural state a harness should recognise as a distinct regime [T2] MIT CTL, §CCC can be negative: Dell in the 1990s, 2016.

## Actionable Levers Versus Accounting Artefacts

For an agent to optimise a business it must know which nodes are set-points it can pull and which are derived quantities it should not treat as targets in themselves. The sources consistently support this separation:

- **Volume, price, mix, and churn** are upstream demand levers; revenue is their product [T3] KPI Tree, "Metric Decomposition," https://kpitree.co/guides/core-concepts/metric-decomposition; [T3] eFutures CFO, §Revenue Variance Decomposition, 2026. Revenue itself is not a lever.
- **Activity drivers** (headcount, setups, batches, purchase orders, customer calls) are the levers of the cost subgraph; cost-pool dollars are outcomes [T2] OpenStax, §Five stages; [T2] Oracle, §Expense drivers, 2024.
- **DIO, DSO, DPO** are policy levers on working-capital stocks [T2] J.P. Morgan, §Ways to optimize CCC, 2025.
- **Capex** is a discretionary lever; **depreciation and amortisation** are accounting allocations of past capex — the D&A line is a non-cash add-back that must be *added back* precisely because it is not a cash flow, and PP&E itself is governed by the identity ending PP&E = beginning PP&E + capex − depreciation [T2] Wall Street Prep, §Capex → PP&E, 2026.
- **Interest burden, tax burden and leverage** in the 5-step DuPont are results of financing and tax policy, not operating levers [T2] CFA Institute, §Extended DuPont, 2013.
- Some accounts should *not* be driver-based at all: not every metric is a driver (drivers must impact processes and outputs, not merely describe them), volatility in a "driver" makes outputs unreliable, correlation must not be mistaken for causation, and controllability must be assessed — some drivers are outside operational control [T2] AFP, §Identify and quantify drivers, 2024.

The strongest structural statement of the artefact problem comes from throughput and REA literature: GAAP-mandated expense recognition (the matching principle) is appropriate for external reporting but does not give an accurate managerial picture of true profitability and cost structure, because it recognises inventory costs late and treats inventory as an asset that TA regards as capital sunk in the system [T2] SAGE, §Throughput Accounting assumptions, 2007; [T2] Goldratt, "Part 14: Inventory: An asset? Or liability?", §Throughput Insights. REA makes the same point constructively: embedding debit-credit at transaction capture codifies a particular reporting view, while deferring it to views over event data "preserves traditional financial reporting options while more faithfully representing economic reality" [T2] MSU Broad, §William McCarthy Hall of Fame, 2026. An agent harness should therefore model *events and policy set-points*, and treat the published statement lines as projections of those events — an accounting artefact being defined as "a statement line with no policy upstream and no cash downstream."

## Conclusion: A Concrete Node-and-Edge Decomposition

Synthesising the above, a generic P&L and balance sheet decompose into the following node-and-edge graph. **Lever nodes** (dark) are upstream set-points an agent may optimise; **stock nodes** are balance-sheet accumulations; **flow nodes** are statement lines derived algebraically from upstream levers.

**Revenue subgraph.** Revenue = Σ (unit volume × price × mix share) across products/segments; recursively, existing-customer revenue = opening customer stock × renewal rate × (1 + expansion rate), and new-customer revenue = funnel (traffic → leads → opportunities → closes) × average deal size [T3] KPI Tree, §Revenue decomposition, https://kpitree.co/guides/core-concepts/metric-decomposition. Price, volume and mix are separate lever branches per price–volume–mix analysis [T3] eFutures CFO, §Revenue variance decomposition, 2026; public-company practice confirms the same vocabulary in published revenue bridges [T2] JELD-WEN, §Revenue Bridge, 2026.

**COGS subgraph.** COGS = Σ unit volume × (material cost per unit + conversion cost per unit), where conversion overhead is driven by activity pools: order cost = purchase orders × cost per order; setup cost = batches × cost per batch; inspection = batch level; energy/direct labour = unit level; facility costs are a step function of capacity, not volume [T2] LibreTexts, §Hierarchy of Costs, 2023; [T2] OpenStax, §Activity pools and cost drivers, 2019.

**Opex subgraph.** SG&A = headcount-driven (FTE × avg salary × benefit/tax multipliers), activity-driven (events × cost per event, campaigns × cost per campaign, calls × cost per call, square footage × cost per sq ft) [T2] Oracle, §Expense drivers, 2024. R&D similarly heads × rate. Within opex live the unit-economics feeds: CAC (new-customer S&M per cohort) and the gross-margin denominator of LTV [T2] The SaaS CFO, §CAC, 2022.

**Working-capital subgraph.** AR = DSO × revenue/365; Inventory = DIO × COGS/365; AP = DPO × COGS/365; CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO is the summary edge; changes in these stocks bridge to operating cash flow [T2] J.P. Morgan, §How to calculate the CCC, 2025; [T2] Oracle, §Balance sheet drivers, 2024.

**Fixed-asset and financing subgraphs.** PP&E = prior PP&E + capex − depreciation (each from the PP&E schedule); depreciation and interest are feeding edges into the income statement; debt = drawdowns − repayments with interest expense fed back; cash is the reconciling plug, negative plug = revolver [T2] A Simple Model, §PP&E Schedule and §Debt Schedule, 2022; [T2] Financial Edge, §Cash and revolver, 2024.

**Constraint and time layers.** Throughput, inventory/investment and operating expense define the constraint layer (bottleneck capacity as a capacity edge); CCC defines the liquidity time-structure [T2] SAGE, §Definitions, 2007; [T2] Allianz Trade, 2026-07-16. The system closes through the identities A = L + E, retained earnings accumulation, and the cash-flow reconciliation, all of which are invariants an agent harness must enforce [T2] Financial Edge, §Balancing the Balance Sheet, 2020; [T2] Wall Street Prep, §Retained earnings as the centerpiece, 2026.

This decomposition is the base layer of the business-as-machine model: a directed graph whose lever nodes are few, whose statement lines are all recomputed, and whose edges are the causal and identity relationships documented above. Every higher layer of abstraction — value chain, capabilities, experiments — ultimately writes back onto this graph through its levers.
