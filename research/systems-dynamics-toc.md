# Systems Dynamics, Stock-and-Flow, and the Theory of Constraints: Modelling the Firm as a Dynamic Machine

## The firm as a stock-and-flow machine

The deepest formal bridge between accounting and dynamic modelling is that a business is already represented, in its financial statements, as a system of stocks and flows. Forrester argued that "all systems, everywhere, consist of these two kinds of concepts—stocks and flows—and none other," and observed that this is exactly the structure of a corporate report: "All numbers on the balance sheet are stocks representing accumulations that have evolved over time. The profit and loss statement represents the flows that cause the stocks to change" [T2] Forrester, *Some Basic Concepts in System Dynamics* (D-4894), section "The feedback system," ¶1. This is a powerful starting point for any agentic business model: every node on the transformation graph is a stock (cash, inventory, receivables, plant, brand equity, trained staff), and every edge is a flow that adds to or drains one stock and typically draws down another [T2] Forrester D-4894, "Generic Structure of a Business," ¶1. Because stocks accumulate and flows are rates, the same accounting identities that a P&L/balance-sheet model encodes can be compiled directly into a simulation.

Sterman generalised this beyond tangible items: "The resource-based view expanded the definition of a firm's resources beyond tangible stocks such as plant, equipment, cash, and other traditional balance sheet items to include less obvious but more important stocks underlying firm capabilities, such as employee skills, customer loyalty, and other forms of intangible human, social, and political capital" [T1] Sterman 2001, "System Dynamics Modeling," section "Stocks and Flows," ¶1. The asset-stock view of competitive advantage gives this idea an economic foundation. Dierickx and Cool show that strategic resources are accumulated, not bought, and that their sustainability depends on properties of the accumulation process: time-compression diseconomies, asset-mass efficiencies, interconnectedness of asset stocks, asset erosion, and causal ambiguity [T1] Dierickx & Cool 1989, p. 1504, Abstract, ¶1; p. 1506 "Accumulation of Asset Stocks," ¶2–3. Their bathtub metaphor—inflow, stock, leak—is the same stock-and-flow primitive Forrester used [T1] Dierickx & Cool 1989, p. 1506, ¶3.

The practical implication for a business graph is that optimisation targets divide into (a) **flow levers** (pricing, hiring, ad spend, collection speed) that move quickly and (b) **stock levers** (capacity, reputation, debt) that respond slowly and often with nonlinear lags. Dierickx and Cool's "time compression diseconomies" warns that pouring money into a strategic asset stream faster than its accumulation rate allows yields lower-quality, higher-cost stock [T1] Dierickx & Cool 1989, p. 1506, ¶4.

## Feedback loops, delays, and the counterintuitive firm

Sterman's synthesis reduces every system to two loop types: "positive (or self-reinforcing) and negative (or self-correcting)" [T1] Sterman 2001, "Feedback," ¶1. Reinforcing loops (word-of-mouth growth, learning curves, network externalities) amplify whatever is happening; balancing loops (market saturation, competitor entry, capacity limits) push back toward a goal [T1] Sterman 2001, "Feedback," ¶1–2. The critical modelling problem is that "when multiple loops interact, it is generally impossible to determine what the dynamics will be by intuition. When intuition fails, we must turn to computer simulation" [T1] Sterman 2001, "Feedback," ¶2. Because delays separate cause from effect, managers systematically mis-infer the structure of the systems they run.

Sterman reports that on the simplest possible stock-and-flow task—inferring a bathtub's fill trajectory from inflow and outflow graphs—"the average performance of graduate students at an elite business school was only 46%" [T1] Sterman 2001, "Stocks and Flows," ¶1. Sterman's beer-distribution experiments show the same bias with higher stakes: decision-makers "underweight the supply line" of orders placed but not yet received, which is sufficient to generate the bullwhip effect in both labs and real chains [T1] Sterman 1989, *Management Science*, Abstract, ¶1; [T1] Croson, Donohue, Katok & Sterman 2005, Abstract, ¶1. Strikingly, the bullwhip "persists" even when all operational causes are eliminated and demand is constant and publicly known, pointing to behavioural causes—bounded rationality and coordination risk [T1] Croson et al. 2005, Abstract, ¶1 and "Introduction," ¶3. Exposing real-time inventory information reduces but does not remove amplification [T1] Croson et al. 2005, "Results," ¶1–2.

For a business graph, delays are first-class edges, not rounding errors. An agent that treats a sales spike as a signal to add capacity, ignoring supply-line and hiring/training lags, will reproduce classic oscillation [T2] Sterman, *Business Dynamics* (2000), ch. 17 "Supply Chains and the Origin of Oscillations," contents. Senge's "systems laws" encode the resulting pathologies—today's problems come from yesterday's solutions; the harder you push the harder the system pushes back; behaviour grows better before it grows worse; cause and effect are not closely related in time and space [T2] Senge, *The Fifth Discipline* (1990), ch. 4 "Laws of the Fifth Discipline," pp. 57–67. These are the failure modes an optimisation loop must price in when it proposes experiments.

## Archetypes and leverage points as diagnostic targets

Senge's system archetypes turn recurring loop-and-delay structures into named diagnostic patterns. [T2] Senge 1990, ch. 6 and Appendix 2. The canonical eight are Fixes That Fail, Shifting the Burden, Limits to Success, Drifting Goals, Growth and Underinvestment, Success to the Successful, Escalation, and Tragedy of the Commons [T2] Kim & Anderson, *Systems Archetype Basics* (Pegasus), "Who's On First?", ¶1. **Limits to Success** combines a driving reinforcing loop with a limiting balancing loop; the leverage is never on the growth engine—"the leverage is never on the reinforcing loop. It is on the limiting constraint"—and pushing harder on the engine before the limit is relaxed produces overshoot or collapse [T3] Cloudstreet, *Systems Thinking*, ch. 8 "Limits to Growth," ¶4. **Shifting the Burden** has competing symptomatic and fundamental loops, where a symptomatic fix atrophies the capability needed for the fundamental solution—the classic outsourcing-a-core-competency trap [T2] Kim & Anderson, "Shifting the Burden," "The Systemic Structure," ¶3; [T2] *The Systems Thinker*, "Boiled Through Outsourcing," V10N5, ¶2. **Growth and Underinvestment** lowers performance standards to justify not investing, which lowers performance further [T2] Kim & Anderson, "Growth and Underinvestment," ¶1.

These patterns map cleanly onto an intervention hierarchy. Meadows' leverage-point list ranks twelve places to intervene in increasing order of effectiveness: constants/parameters (12) → buffers (11) → stock-and-flow structure (10) → delays (9) → balancing-loop strength (8) → reinforcing-loop gain (7) → information flows (6) → rules/incentives (5) → self-organisation (4) → goals (3) → paradigms (2) → transcending paradigms (1) [T2] Meadows 1999, "Places to Intervene in a System," ¶2. The recurring empirical finding is that "organizations and policymakers naturally reach for the lowest-leverage interventions (adjusting numbers, adding resources) and systematically avoid higher-leverage interventions" [T3] Cloudstreet ch. 8, "Closing," ¶2. For an agentic harness this is an explicit search-space ordering: parameter tuning is cheap and weak; changing information flows, rules, and goals is powerful but resists change [T2] Meadows 1999, entries 6–3. A business model that only exposes financial parameters leaves the highest-leverage nodes—goals, decision rules, who sees what information—unmodelled.

Archetypes also define **where the node is that must be attacked**. Limits to Growth says find the limiting balancing loop; Shifting the Burden says build the fundamental capability even while the quick fix runs; Fixes That Fail says surface the delayed side-effect loop [T2] Kim & Anderson, "Fixes That Fail," "The Systemic Structure," ¶1; [T3] LifeOS, *SystemsThinking/Archetypes.md*, entries 1–3.

## Beer's VSM and Team Syntegrity: the control architecture of the machine

Stock-and-flow models say what the business *contains*; Beer's Viable System Model (VSM) says what any system must *have* to remain viable. The VSM holds that a viable system comprises five necessary and sufficient subsystems: System 1, the primary activities performing the key transformation; System 2, coordination damping oscillations between them; System 3, operational control and resource optimisation; System 4, intelligence looking outward and forward; and System 5, policy and identity [T3] Wikipedia, "Viable system model," "Systems," ¶1; [T2] *The Viable Systems Model Guide* (Waters), "Five systems," table. Viability is recursive: "In a recursive organizational structure any viable system contains, and is contained in, a viable system" [T2] Beer, *The Viable System Model: Its Provenance, Development, Methodology and Pathology*, "Recursive system theorem," p. 118. Control is regulated by Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety—the variety the system must absorb must match its environment's—and by algedonic ("pain/pleasure") alerts that bypass normal channels when performance breaches thresholds [T3] Wikipedia, "Viable system model," §§"Requisite Variety," "Algedonic alerts."

For a business graph, the VSM supplies the *control edges*. System 1 nodes are the value-transforming units; System 2 edges are the coordination/inventory-buffering links that Sterman showed are essential to damp oscillation; System 3 is the resource-allocation node; System 4 is the environment-sensing/forecasting node; System 5 sets the objective function and constraints (mission, values, brand). Beer's later experiments found a common pathology: management believes someone else is doing System 4, so "if 4 doesn't effectively exist... this huge superstructure 5 collapses into 3" and the whole becomes reactive [T2] Beer, *The Falcondale Collection*, Session 08, ¶ on System 4.

Because the VSM is abstract, Beer designed two facilitation technologies to operate it. **Team Syntegrity / Syntegration** is a non-hierarchical protocol for 10–42 people in which 12 topics are mapped onto an icosahedron, each participant serving as member, critic, and observer, to reach collective decisions on identity and policy (operationalising the System 3/4/5 homeostat) [T2] Metaphorum, "Syntegration—Team Syntegrity," ¶1–3; [T2] Beer, *Beyond Dispute: The Invention of Team Syntegrity* (1994), as described in [T2] Truss, Cullen & Leonard, "The Coherent Architecture of Team Syntegrity," ¶1. The brief's reference to "STORM" is best read not as a Beer artefact but as the **Storm Methodology**, a group-modelling variant combining stakeholder value mapping, group model building with causal loop diagrams, and a value scan for cost-benefit/ROI estimation [T1] *An Exploration of the Added Value of Systems Analysis and System Dynamics* (System Dynamics Conference 2012), "The Storm Methodology," ¶1–2. It shares with Syntegration the function of structuring collective model-building, but with SD quantification rather than geometric facilitation.

The VSM's empirical standing is stronger than its reputation suggests. Schwaninger reports five case studies, including a Swiss insurance group reorganised around customer problems: the transformation was completed in six months, "realized smoothly and without noteworthy resistance," and a follow-up twenty years later credited the project as the bedrock of the group's rise [T1] Schwaninger 2006, *Kybernetes* 35(7/8), "Power of the viable system model," pp. 955–958. He argues the model "has not been falsified but, on the contrary, corroborated by the growing empirical evidence from VSM applications" [T1] Schwaninger 2006, p. 956, ¶2. Independent work develops constitutive rules for its competent use [T1] Hoverstadt & Loh, *Constitutive rules for guiding the use of the viable system model* (EJOR 2020), Abstract, ¶1.

## Theory of constraints and throughput accounting: finding the binding node

Where VSM diagnoses control architecture, Goldratt's Theory of Constraints (TOC) diagnoses the **binding node** in the transformation graph. Its premise: "any manageable system is limited in achieving more of its goals by a very small number of constraints," and the rate of goal achievement (throughput) can only rise by increasing flow through the constraint [T3] Wikipedia, "Theory of constraints," ¶1–3. The Five Focusing Steps are: identify the constraint; exploit it; subordinate everything else to it; elevate it; and repeat without letting inertia become the new constraint [T3] Wikipedia, "Theory of constraints," "Five focusing steps," ¶1–2. The operations method is Drum-Buffer-Rope: the drum is the bottleneck's rate, the buffer protects it from starvation, and the rope releases work at the drum's pace to prevent excess WIP [T3] Wikipedia, "Theory of constraints," "Drum-buffer-rope," ¶1–2; [T2] SAP, *Theory of Constraints* help document, "Drum-Buffer-Rope principle," ¶1–4.

Throughput Accounting (TA) translates TOC into a P&L-compatible measurement layer. It uses three measures—**Throughput** (sales minus totally variable cost), **Inventory/Investment** (money tied up), and **Operating Expense** (money spent turning investment into throughput)—and deliberately refuses to allocate fixed overhead to products [T3] Wikipedia, "Throughput accounting," "The three measures," ¶1–2; [T2] *Cost & Profitability*, "Throughput Accounting," ¶1–2. The distinctive decision rule is to rank products by **throughput per unit of the constraint** (e.g., throughput per bottleneck-minute) rather than by unit margin, because a higher unit margin can consume disproportionately more of the scarce resource [T2] *Cost & Profitability*, "Throughput accounting," ¶3–4, worked example. This is exactly the graph-optimisation logic the business-as-machine model needs: value each candidate initiative by its throughput contribution per unit of the binding constraint, not by its standalone financial return.

TOC also extends beyond the factory. It generalises to project management as Critical Chain, and its "thinking process" treats constraints as potentially policy- or market-based rather than physical [T1] Lizarralde Aiastui 2020, *Application of TOC-DBR to Make-To-Order Manufacturing*, "Introduction," ¶1–2; [T2] Goldratt Marketing, *DBR, Buffer Management, and VATI Flow Classification*, product description. The VATI classification (V, A, T, I plant types) is a structural taxonomy of flow topologies—directly analogous to identifying the shape of the transformation subgraph [T2] Goldratt Marketing, "Topics," ¶1.

The evidence base is real but uneven. Panizzolo's survey of 61 European manufacturers found Drum-Buffer-Rope, non-constraint resources held with excess capacity, and constraint-optimised master scheduling the most effective practices across cost, due-date, lead-time, inventory, and cycle-time performance [T1] Panizzolo 2016, Abstract, ¶1. Mabin and Balderstone found around 80% of companies using TOC-DBR reported improvements in meeting delivery deadlines [T1] Mabin & Balderstone 2003, as summarised in Lizarralde Aiastui 2020, "Benefits," ¶1. A seven-year case at India's largest lock manufacturer reported inventory reductions, near-elimination of stock-outs, distributor inventory turns "more than tripled," sales nearly tripling, and profits doubling across the Great Recession [T1] Modi, Lowalekar & Bhatta 2019, *International Journal of Production Research* 57(11), Abstract, ¶1. Simulation of a real Indian auto-components firm shows throughput rising and lead time falling as bottlenecks are relaxed and shifted [T1] *Application of TOC Strategy Using Simulation* (IEOM 2021), "Results," ¶1–2. The caveat is built into TOC itself: every time a constraint is broken a new one appears, and constraints may lie outside the firm at demand [T1] Lizarralde Aiastuti 2020, "Shifting of Bottleneck," ¶1.

## Where system-dynamics models of firms actually improved decisions—and where they failed

Evidence that SD has improved real corporate decisions clusters in project and strategy work. Lyneis, Cooper and Els report that SD models "have proven their value in contributing to significantly improved project performance" [T1] Lyneis, Cooper & Els 2001, *System Dynamics Review* 17(3), Abstract, ¶1. The most quantified commercial result is Fluor Corporation's use of the same project model on "over 100 projects"; "Quantitative business benefits exceed $800 million to date for Fluor and our clients" [T1] Godlewski, Lee & Cooper 2012, *Interfaces* 42(1), Abstract, ¶1. In strategy, the Risch–Sterman pulp-and-paper study used causal-loop analysis to explain why a newly acquired mill kept losing money: management "underestimated a variety of side effects of the new strategy, both at the market level and in the mill" [T1] Risch, Sterman & Troyano-Bermúdez 1995, *System Dynamics Review* 11(4), Abstract, ¶1. Management flight simulators have taught growth dynamics at scale, with "hundreds of plays" generating lessons about balancing resources and anticipating limits [T1] Lyneis 1998, *Learning to Manage Growth* (System Dynamics Conference), Abstract, ¶1.

The failure evidence is equally instructive. Eskinasi and Fokkema document an SD consulting product whose adaptation "finally had to be terminated due to negative business results," attributing the failure to archetype-like dynamics such as Shifting the Burden [T1] Eskinasi & Fokkema 2006, *Systems Research and Behavioral Science* 23(4), Abstract, ¶1. Größler distinguishes low-impact projects (no changed policies) from those where policy changed but SD was not institutionalised, and argues the fix is to treat modelling as an organisational intervention with stakeholder management [T1] Größler 2007, *Systems Research and Behavioral Science* 24, "System dynamics projects as organizational interventions," ¶1–2. A five-case study at BMW found SD helped managers build richer cognitive representations but produced a "cognition gap" between modellers and non-modellers [T1] Springer/Electronic Markets 2019, *System Dynamics for corporate business model innovation*, Abstract, ¶1 and "Discussion," ¶1.

The pattern is consistent: SD models improve decisions when embedded in a change process with sponsorship, participation, and stakeholder management—and fail when technically sound but detached from the decisions they were meant to inform.

## Data requirements and modelling constraints

Forrester prioritised three types of data: "numerical, written and mental data, in increasing order of importance" [T1] Houghton et al. 2014, *A Survey of Methods for Data Inclusion in System Dynamics Models*, ¶1. The most valuable inputs are the **mental models** of decision-makers, then documented policies and narrative, with time-series numbers least important for structure though essential for calibration. SD can represent "soft" factors such as morale and service quality as ordinary feedback variables, avoiding discrete-event simulation's need to pre-quantify them [T1] Mayo & Wichmann 2003, *Tutorial on Business and Market Modeling*, "Comparison," table.

Calibration and validation set the practical bar. SD tests whether equation structure can reproduce "typically 3–5 years" of history "without direct computational use of the data" [T1] Mayo & Wichmann 2003, "Use of data," ¶1. Validation is a gradient: Schwaninger catalogues structure, behaviour, and policy tests; Barlas adds Taguchi-style robust parameter testing; Wakeland and Hoarfrost found extreme-condition and family-member tests gave the best benefit-to-cost ratio, contradicting over-reliance on sensitivity analysis [T2] Schwaninger, *System Dynamics Modeling: Validation for Quality Assurance*, Appendix; [T1] Barlas 2011, Abstract, ¶1; [T1] Wakeland & Hoarfrost, Abstract, ¶1.

Three data thresholds follow. **Financial stock/flow data** (ledgers, XBRL) initialise the accounting layer. **Operational flow data** (capacity, cycle times, churn, CAC/LTV) parameterise transformation edges and locate constraints—TOC needs capacity and constraint-time data, not absorbed product costs [T2] *Cost & Profitability*, "Throughput Accounting," ¶2. **Behavioural/policy data** (decision rules, incentives, delays) are most important and least numerical, captured via interviews and group model building [T1] Mayo & Wichmann 2003, "Document the Key Cause Effect Relationships," ¶1; [T1] Storm Methodology 2012, "Description," ¶1–2.

## Synthesis: mapping the approaches onto the business graph

Stock-and-flow modelling supplies the **nodes and edges** (assets, liabilities, transformation flows) plus conservation and time-integration. Causal-loop/archetype analysis and Meadows' leverage hierarchy supply the **diagnostic and search-ordering** layer: the binding lever is more often a delay, information flow, rule, or goal than a parameter [T2] Meadows 1999, list; [T2] Senge 1990, ch. 6. Beer's VSM supplies the **control architecture**—which nodes must exist (operations, coordination, control, intelligence, policy) and how requisite variety and algedonic signals connect them—recursively, so the same model applies at team, unit, or group level [T3] Wikipedia, "Viable system model," §§"Systems," "Recursion." TOC and throughput accounting supply the **bottleneck-targeting and valuation rule**: find the constraint, exploit and elevate it, and rank initiatives by throughput per unit of the constraint [T3] Wikipedia, "Throughput accounting," ¶1; [T2] ACCA, *Throughput accounting*, ¶ on throughput per bottleneck. An optimisation loop should therefore move flows through the current constraint, protect it with buffers, watch for it shifting, and reserve the highest-leverage interventions for the loops archetype recognition identifies as binding.

The honest limits remain. Human intuition about stocks, flows, and delays is demonstrably poor [T1] Sterman 2001, "Stocks and Flows," ¶1; [T1] Sterman 1989, Abstract, ¶1. SD models succeed only when embedded in decision processes with stakeholder engagement and sponsorship, and can fail or create cognition gaps when they do not [T1] Eskinasi & Fokkema 2006, Abstract, ¶1; [T1] Größler 2007, ¶1–2; [T1] Springer/Electronic Markets 2019, Abstract, ¶1. TOC benefits are context-dependent and the constraint migrates after each intervention [T1] Panizzolo 2016, "Conclusions," ¶1; [T1] Lizarralde Aiastui 2020, "Shifting of Bottleneck," ¶1. A harness should therefore treat every model as provisionally wrong and learn from the side-effect loops it reveals [T1] Sterman 2002, *All models are wrong*, Abstract, ¶1.
