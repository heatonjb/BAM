# Towards a Transformation Algebra

## The question: a coherent algebra, or a construction?

No single published formalism composes the candidate ingredients — throughput accounting, value-stream metrics, cost-driver rates, queueing relations, object-centric process mining, and stock-and-flow dynamics — into one algebra over a business graph. Each is a partial view: theory of constraints (TOC) supplies money primitives but no clock; system dynamics supplies conservation but no cost-driver rates; value-stream mapping and queueing supply time but no valuation; time-driven activity-based costing (TDABC) prices time but is explicitly a costing tool, not a valuation standard; object-centric performance analysis supplies multi-object time metrics but no money. They are nonetheless *compatible in principle* because they rest on two shared invariants: stock–flow conservation and Little's law. What is missing is a single typed graph with unit checking, one valuation convention, and composition operators. That algebra must be constructed, not borrowed wholesale.

The conceptual scaffolding already exists. Forrester argues that "all systems, everywhere, consist of these two kinds of concepts—stocks and flows—and none other," and that a balance sheet is a page of stocks while the P&L is a page of flows ([T2] Forrester 2009, "Some Basic Concepts in System Dynamics," §Closed-loop Structure, lines 15–18). The REA (Resources-Events-Agents) accounting ontology, standardized as ISO/IEC 15944-4, gives every economic event a duality — a giving and a receiving side — and defines the primitive classes (economic resource, economic event, agent, commitment) needed for a machine-readable transaction graph ([T2] ISO/IEC 15944-4:2015, Annex B, lines 3–8). What neither provides is a quantitative rate/time/cost algebra over those primitives. Little's law, L = λW, is the bridge that turns stocks into times ([T1] Little & Graves 2008, "Little's Law," p. 93, ¶2). The rest of this document tests whether the pieces can be welded to that bridge.

## The candidate ingredients and their native units

**Throughput accounting (TOC).** Three operational measures: Throughput T, the rate at which the system generates money through sales; Investment/Inventory I, money invested in things intended to sell; and Operating Expense OE, money spent turning I into T. Global measures are Net Profit NP = T − OE, Return on Investment ROI = NP/I, Productivity = T/OE, and Inventory turnover = T/I ([T2] TOCICO 2018, "Back to Basics TOC: Throughput Accounting," §Basics; [T2] IMA 1999, "Theory of Constraints Management System Fundamentals," §Operational Measures). Throughput subtracts only totally variable costs (TVC), usually raw materials, from revenue ([T2] IMA 1999, §Throughput Accounting; [T3] Wikipedia, "Throughput accounting," §Definitions). Units: currency per period (T, OE), currency (I).

**Value-stream mapping (VSM).** Takt time = available production time ÷ customer demand; cycle time (CT) = time between one completed unit and the next at a process; lead time (LT) = total elapsed order-to-delivery time; %C&A = percent complete and accurate per step; rolled first-pass yield RFPY = Π %C&A over steps; Activity Ratio / Process Cycle Efficiency = process time ÷ lead time ([T2] Minitab Engage, "Value stream map and timeline calculations," §Customer demand and takt time; [T2] IBM DevOps Loop 2.0.2, "Displaying the metrics on the value stream," §Metrics). Units: min/unit, days/unit, dimensionless percentages. Practitioner guidance stresses that "takt time is not a cycle time": takt is a demand-driven pacemaker, CT is a supply-side rate ([T3] Symestic, "Value Stream Mapping," https://www.symestic.com/en-us/what-is/value-stream-mapping).

**Queueing relations.** Little's law L = λW states the long-run average number of items in a stationary system equals the average arrival rate times the average time in system; the operations form is TH = WIP/CT ([T1] Little & Graves 2008, p. 93, ¶2; [T1] Little 2011, "Little's Law as Viewed on Its 50th Anniversary," §3, ¶1). The G/G/1 approximation, Kingman's formula or the VUT equation, gives mean queue delay E(W_q) ≈ (ρ/(1−ρ))·((c_a²+c_s²)/2)·τ, where ρ is utilization, c the coefficients of variation of arrivals and service, and τ the mean service time ([T1] Chin 2009, "Queueing Theory and Process Flow Performance," 17th IGLC, p. 251, ¶2; [T3] AllAboutLean, "The Kingman Formula," https://www.allaboutlean.com/kingman-formula). Units: time; coefficients dimensionless.

**TDABC / ABC.** Capacity cost rate CCR = cost of capacity supplied ÷ practical capacity ([T2] Kaplan & Anderson 2004, "Time-Driven Activity-Based Costing," HBS WP 04-045, §Unit cost, lines 8–11). Unit cost = CCR × unit time, and a time equation estimates duration as T = β₀ + β₁X₁ + … + βᵢXᵢ, so heterogeneous work is priced by drivers rather than subjective allocation ([T2] Kaplan & Anderson 2004, §Activity Unit Time; [T1] Stouthuysen et al. 2017 review in *Health Policy*, §3.5.6). Crucially, TDABC distinguishes capacity *supplied* from capacity *used*, surfacing unused-capacity cost rather than burying it in product cost ([T2] Kaplan & Anderson 2004, §Unused Capacity). Units: currency/time (CCR), currency/unit (activity cost), time (unit time).

**Object-centric process mining.** Case-centric mining forces every event into one case ID; when events touch multiple objects, waiting-time measures become "misleading or even incorrect." Using object-centric Petri nets, OPerA defines synchronization time (waiting to gather multiple objects), pooling time (waiting to pool objects of one type), and lagging time (delay caused by one object lagging others), with flow time = sojourn time + synchronization time, and synchronization = pooling + lagging ([T1] Park, Adams & van der Aalst 2022, "OPerA: Object-Centric Performance Analysis," arXiv:2204.10662, §1 abstract, ¶3; §3, pp. 3, 11). The OCEL standard records events linked to arbitrarily many objects and types ([T1] van der Aalst 2022, "Object-Centric Process Mining: An Introduction," §3; [T2] Microsoft Learn, "Overview of object-centric process mining," §How OCPM models this process).

**System dynamics.** Stocks S(t) accumulate flows: dS/dt = inflow − outflow, with flows as rates and feedback loops around them ([T2] Forrester 2009, §Closed-loop Structure; [T1] Baez et al. 2023, "Compositional Modeling with Stock and Flow Diagrams," arXiv:2205.08373, §2). Units: stock in units, flow in units/time.

**Working-capital and unit-economics bridges.** CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO, where DIO = avg inventory ÷ COGS × 365, DSO = avg receivables ÷ revenue × 365, DPO = avg payables ÷ COGS × 365 ([T3] CFA Institute, "A Look at the Cash Conversion Cycle," https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/insideinvesting/2013/05/21/a-look-at-the-cash-conversion-cycle/; [T2] J.P. Morgan, "Working Capital Index," 2019, §CCC). Unit economics: contribution margin CM = ARPU × gross margin; LTV = CM ÷ churn (or a discounted cohort sum); CAC payback = CAC ÷ (ARPU × gross margin); Magic Number = ΔRevenue ÷ S&M spend; net revenue retention (NRR) = (starting MRR + expansion − contraction − churn) ÷ starting MRR ([T3] The SaaS CFO, "How I Calculate the CAC Payback Period," https://www.thesaascfo.com/cac-payback-period/; [T3] CloudZero, "The Complete SaaS Unit Economics Guide," https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/saas-unit-economics; [T2] Drivetrain × Benchmarkit, "B2B SaaS Performance Metrics Benchmarks 2025," pp. 2–4).

## Where they compose, and where they collide

**Composition points.** Little's law is the Rosetta stone that makes VSM and TOC speak the same language: TOC's stock I and VSM's WIP are the same L, queueing's W is VSM lead time, and TOC's throughput T is the flow rate λ. TDABC's unit time is exactly VSM's process time; the capacity cost rate simply prices that time, so one node can carry both a time parameter and a money/time parameter. The cash-conversion cycle is Little's law applied to cash: DIO is the flow-time of inventory in days ([T3] CFA Institute, §DIO). TOC's throughput margin is the system-level unit economics, and LTV is that margin integrated over a customer lifetime — both are "T per customer." Object-centric metrics extend this to transformations that consume several input objects at once: the "wait for all inputs" is synchronization time, invisible to case-centric Little's law ([T1] Park et al. 2022, p. 3, ¶2).

**Collision 1 — valuation (GAAP vs. throughput).** Absorption (GAAP) costing capitalizes fixed overhead into inventory; throughput accounting values inventory at TVC only. The two produce different net income and the difference is mechanical: under absorption costing, profit rises when inventory rises while cash falls, and falls when inventory falls while cash rises ([T3] "Theory of Constraints Bottom Line Accounting for Change," http://dbrmfg.co.nz; [T2] TOCICO 2018, §Cost per Unit Distortion). The reconciliation identity is NI_GAAP = NP_TA + ΔInventory × overhead-per-unit ([T3] dbrmfg.co.nz). Mixing the two valuations without this bridge double counts or silently loses overhead.

**Collision 2 — allocation vs. no-allocation.** TOC holds that "allocation = distortion, thus the solution must not allocate" ([T2] TOCICO 2018, §CPU Summary). ABC and TDABC deliberately assign resource cost to activities and products. If an analyst sums allocated product costs *and* charges the same shared resource's period cost to OE, the resource is counted twice. TDABC's supplied-vs-used split is the escape hatch: assign only *used* capacity to cost objects and report *unused* capacity separately ([T2] Kaplan & Anderson 2004, §Unused Capacity). This is not academic: a comparative study finds ABC and TOC can lead to *contradictory product-mix decisions*, proving the two models are not interchangeable ([T1] Kee & Schmidt 2000, *IJPE* 63(1):1–17, abstract, p. 1, ¶1). In high-demand-variation settings, traditional cost methods can overstate net income by up to 70% relative to throughput accounting ([T1] *Expert Systems with Applications* 2015, "Throughput accounting and performance of a supply chain," §Abstract).

**Collision 3 — time semantics.** VSM's "cycle time" (touch time) is not Little's CT (sojourn time including queue), and neither is takt (demand pace). Summing them double counts. The Activity Ratio already embeds queue time by dividing process time by lead time ([T2] Minitab Engage, §Timeline summary box).

**Collision 4 — variability and yield.** RFPY (yield) and the squared coefficient of variation c_a² (arrival variability) both encode rework and disorder. Applying both to the same flow double counts variability; one should be a unit-demand multiplier (1/RFPY) and the other a queue-CV input. Kingman's VUT is itself an approximation, accurate mainly near saturation, not an exact law ([T3] AllAboutLean, https://www.allaboutlean.com/kingman-formula; [T1] Chin 2009, p. 251, ¶2).

**Collision 5 — steady state.** Little's law holds for long-run averages over a defined boundary in a stationary system; queueing theory "is not focused on what happens in finite intervals" ([T1] Little 2011, §1, ¶4). Accounting-period ratios are snapshots, so L = λW must be applied with an explicit boundary and period, and not to transient windows.

## The consistency rule: one graph, one valuation, conserved flows

A transformation algebra needs three commitments. First, a **typed graph**: nodes are stocks (cash, inventory, WIP, receivables, payables, deferred revenue, property, people/capacity, and intangibles such as brand), and directed edges are transformations carrying parameters (unit time, capacity, yield, variable cost, fixed cost, driver mix). Each edge inherits REA duality: it gives one set of resources and receives another ([T2] ISO/IEC 15944-4:2015, Annex B.2; [T2] van der Aalst 2022, §1). Second, a **single valuation convention** for every stock, so that no resource is both capitalized and expensed. Third, a **conservation axiom**: every unit of a resource either sits in a stock or has traversed an edge, and every dollar is assigned to a period or a stock, never both. These three commitments are what prevent the double counting documented above; the ingredients themselves do not enforce them.

## A minimal consistent set of equations (with units)

Definitions: p_k unit price [currency/unit]; v_k totally variable cost [currency/unit]; τ_j unit time at node j [time/unit]; A_j available capacity time [time/period]; m_j parallel servers; y_j yield [dimensionless]; r_j realized rate [units/period]; I_j stock [units]; W_j flow time [time]; OE fixed operating expense [currency/period]; d discount rate [1/time].

| # | Equation | Units | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | c_j = m_j · A_j / τ_j | units/period | Capacity of node j |
| 2 | ρ_j = r_j / c_j | dimensionless | Utilization |
| 3 | dI_i/dt = Σ_j a_ij r_j − Σ_j b_ij r_j | units/period | Conservation (a, b stoichiometry) |
| 4 | r_out = r_in · y_j ; RFPY = Π_j y_j | units/period; dimensionless | Yield propagation |
| 5 | W_j = I_j / r_j (L = λW) | time | Little's law per node |
| 6 | W_q ≈ (ρ/(1−ρ))·((c_a²+c_s²)/2)·τ ; W = W_q + τ | time | VUT queue delay |
| 7 | W_flow = W_sojourn + W_sync ; W_sync = W_pool + W_lag | time | Object-centric flow time |
| 8 | TH = min_j c_j (or LP max Σ(p_k−v_k)x_k s.t. Σ τ x ≤ A) | units/period | Constraint / mix |
| 9 | T = Σ_k (p_k − v_k) x_k ; NP = T − OE | currency/period | Throughput accounting |
| 10 | ΔNP = ΔT − ΔOE ; ROI = ΔNP / ΔI | currency/period; ratio | Decision rule |
| 11 | NI_GAAP = NP_TA + ΔInventory · o | currency/period | Valuation bridge (o = overhead/unit) |
| 12 | CCR_r = OC_r / PracCap_r ; unit cost = CCR_r · τ | currency/time; currency/unit | TDABC |
| 13 | CM = p − v ; LTV = CM/churn ; payback = CAC/CM | currency; currency; time | Unit economics |
| 14 | CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO | days | Cash cycle |
| 15 | ΔCash = NP − ΔNWC − Capex + Financing | currency/period | Cash bridge |
| 16 | ΔValue = Σ_t [(ΔT_t − ΔOE_t) − ΔI_t · h] / (1+d)^t | currency | Initiative valuation (h = hurdle) |

Equations 3 and 5 are the universal glue; 1, 4, 6, 7 are the flow-time and capacity layer; 9–16 are the money layer. The algebra becomes an algebra, rather than a bag of KPIs, when composition rules are fixed: transformations in series compose yields multiplicatively (y_total = Π y_j) and times additively (τ_total = Σ τ_j) with capacity c_series = min_j c_j; transformations in parallel add capacity (c_parallel = Σ c_j) while pooling variability; a feedback edge turns the static graph into a system-dynamics model where r_j = f(S, params) ([T1] Baez et al. 2023, §2; [T2] Forrester 2009, §Closed-loop Structure). Little and VUT supply the composition rules for time, conservation for quantity, and TDABC for cost — but only the last is easily mis-composed, which is why the valuation axiom must be explicit.

## Empirical data each equation requires

- **Conservation (3) and flows (4, 8):** event logs with quantities, timestamps, resource IDs, and object IDs. Object-centric event logs (OCEL) are the natural substrate because one event can reference many objects, preserving the multi-object relationships that flattened case logs lose ([T1] van der Aalst 2022, §3; [T2] Microsoft Learn, "OCPM process overview," §Process analysis).
- **Capacity and unit time (1, 6):** MES/ERP throughput counts, timesheets, work-sampling, and TDABC time equations calibrated by observation ([T2] Kaplan & Anderson 2004, §Activity Unit Time).
- **Yield and quality (4):** quality systems, rework tickets, and per-step %C&A, from which RFPY is the product ([T2] Minitab Engage, §Timeline summary box).
- **Stock levels and rates (3, 5, 8):** ERP perpetual inventory plus event counts; in steady state, Little's law lets one unknown substitute for another ([T1] Little 2011, §4).
- **Variability inputs (6):** the empirical distributions of inter-arrival and service times, summarized by c_a and c_s ([T2] MIT 15.772J, "Capacity Planning and Production Flow Control," slides 2–9).
- **Money layer (9–16):** price lists and BOM/COGS for p and v; GL fixed accounts for OE; AR/AP/inventory subledgers for DIO/DSO/DPO ([T2] J.P. Morgan, "Working Capital Index," 2019, §Dissecting the CCC); CRM and billing cohorts for CAC, churn, and NRR ([T3] CloudZero, https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/saas-unit-economics); and cost-accounting records for the absorbed overhead rate o.
- **Object-centric time (7):** OCEL logs with object-to-object and event-to-object relations, required to compute pooling and lagging ([T1] Park et al. 2022, pp. 3, 11; [T1] van der Aalst 2022, §3).

## What the algebra must add beyond the ingredients

Three gaps remain after the pieces are assembled. First, **no published work puts cost-driver rates on an object-centric graph**: OPerA delivers synchronization, pooling, and lagging time but no monetary value ([T1] Park et al. 2022, p. 3, ¶2), while TDABC delivers money-per-time but assumes linear, single-activity flows ([T2] Kaplan & Anderson 2004, §Unit cost). Second, **no existing model both enforces a valuation convention and spans cash**: TOC refuses allocation and has no time dimension ([T2] TOCICO 2018, §CPU Summary); system dynamics has both stocks and flows but no driver rates or unit economics ([T2] Forrester 2009). Third, **the ingredients disagree empirically**, as the contradictory ABC-vs-TOC product-mix results show ([T1] Kee & Schmidt 2000, abstract, p. 1, ¶1). A transformation algebra is therefore a construction whose minimal core is: one typed graph (REA primitives), one conservation law (stock-and-flow), one time law (Little, with VUT refinement), one valuation convention (with the GAAP/throughput bridge), and composition operators with unit checking. Because each layer uses different units, benchmarking must be layered over the same nodes — cycle time against peers, capacity cost rate against peers, contribution margin against peers, CCC against peers ([T2] J.P. Morgan, "Working Capital Index," 2019, §Industry Benchmarking; [T2] Drivetrain × Benchmarkit, "B2B SaaS Benchmarks 2025," pp. 2–4) — rather than collapsed prematurely into a single score.
