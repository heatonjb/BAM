# Experiment and Initiative Design Systems

A business-as-machine can be read as a directed graph: nodes are assets, liabilities, and the means of transformation; edges are the transformations that convert one asset into another; and a subset of nodes is sold to produce revenue. Growth is not a single act but the repeated traversal of that graph. The methods in this file are, in effect, the design language for intervening on it — how a strategist, human or agentic, generates candidate interventions, chooses among them, sequences them, and evaluates whether the graph moved as intended. Each method below is mapped to the layer it typically optimises, with its measurement failure modes and its place in a portfolio governed by the constraints (cashflow, legal, intention, values, brand) that bound the machine.

## 1. The Business as Compounding Loops, Not a Funnel

The default mental model, AARRR (Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral, Revenue), is a linear funnel: traffic enters at the top, a share survives each stage, and the rest leaks ([T3] startupfundraising.com, https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/growth-loops-vs-aarrr-funnels-choosing-your-startup-scaling-strategy). The funnel is useful for *diagnosis* — a 60% drop between signup and activation is an actionable finding — and it is the only engine that works on day zero, before there are users to power anything else ([T3] productgrowth.blog, https://www.productgrowth.blog/p/growth-funnels-vs-growth-loops). Its strategic weakness is that nothing flows back to the top: every new customer must be bought again, so customer acquisition cost sets a hard ceiling and channel saturation makes the cost rise as you scale ([T2] Reforge, "Growth Loops are the New Funnels," §"Funnels Operate in One Direction," ¶1–2). The funnel also induces silos: marketing owns acquisition, product owns retention, sales owns revenue, and each optimises its own stage at the expense of the others ([T2] Reforge, "Growth Loops are the New Funnels," §"Funnels Create Functional Silos," ¶1).

The growth-loop model replaces the line with a circuit: an input produces an output that is reinvested into the next cycle. Reforge identifies over 20 loops — viral/referral, content, paid, sales — driving acquisition, retention, defensibility, or efficiency, and argues the fastest-growing products are powered by just one or two dominant loops that transition over time ([T2] Reforge, "Growth Loops are the New Funnels," §"Loops are the New Funnels," ¶2–4). The loop reframes investment: an initiative that yields 500 users once is inferior to one yielding 20 users that grows 10% week-on-week, because the compounding is the point ([T2] Reforge, "Growth Loops are the New Funnels," §"Loops Change How You Make Investment Decisions," ¶2). In graph terms, the loop is a cycle in the transformation graph whose edge weights (invite rate, output-sharing rate, content-indexing rate) determine whether the cycle closes or decays into a decorative funnel ([T3] productgrowth.blog, https://www.productgrowth.blog/p/growth-funnels-vs-growth-loops).

Reforge's **Racecar Growth Framework** decomposes the machine into four parts that map cleanly onto graph elements: the **Engine** (self-sustaining loops), **Turbo Boosts** (one-off events that accelerate but do not persist, e.g. PR), **Lubricants** (efficiencies that raise loop throughput — conversion, activation, retention), and **Fuel** (capital, content, or users the engine consumes) ([T2] Reforge, "The Racecar Growth Framework," §"The Racecar Growth Framework," ¶1–2). Of these, retention is the most important lubricant because it feeds every other input: improving retention improves the rest of the funnel ([T2] Reforge, "The Racecar Growth Framework," §"Lubricants," ¶3).

The financial scoreboard for the loop layer is **growth accounting**, which decomposes net change into mutually exclusive movements rather than reporting a single growth rate ([T2] Jonathan Hsu, "Accounting for Revenue Growth," ¶1–4). For revenue: `ΔMRR = new + resurrected + expansion − churned − contraction`, and the **quick ratio** = gross additions ÷ gross losses, with a widely cited benchmark above 4.0 for enterprise SaaS ([T2] Wall Street Prep, "SaaS Quick Ratio," §"Formula," ¶1; [T2] Hsu, ¶6). The ratio alone is misleading without its *mix*, because a high ratio driven entirely by expansion in a business with no new logos is a ceiling approaching, not health ([T2] Strategy of Finance, "Growth Accounting," §"The standard," ¶4). A compact formula version of the whole machine — the "business equation" — expresses ARR as new seats × price, plus expansion, reactivation, minus churn and contraction, forcing every lever to be named and owned ([T2] Lenny's Newsletter, "How to describe your business as an equation," §"B2B SaaS," ¶2).

## 2. Mapping Interventions to Graph Nodes and Layers

Different methods attach to different node types. Treating the machine as layered — L0 financial (P&L/balance-sheet items), L1 growth engine (acquisition/retention/monetisation stocks and flows), L2 loops (cycles), L3 capabilities/processes (value-chain steps), L4 team/architecture — clarifies which method optimises what:

| Method | Primary graph target | What it optimises | Anchor metric |
|---|---|---|---|
| AARRR funnel | Nodes along the acquisition→revenue path | Stage conversion rates (diagnosis) | Activation rate, CAC, churn |
| Growth loop / Racecar | Cycles (edges) in the engine | Reinvestment and compounding | k-factor, loop output share ([T2] Reforge 2018, §"Loops are the New Funnels") |
| Growth accounting & cohort P&L | Retention nodes (stock/flow) | Quality and composition of growth | Quick ratio, NRR, CAC payback ([T2] Hsu, ¶4; [T3] productphilosophy.com) |
| North Star metric tree | Output node + input nodes | Alignment from work to value | NSM + 4–5 input metrics ([T2] Reforge, "How to Choose & Measure North Star Metrics," §"Three primary North Star Metrics") |
| A/B/n test | A randomised conversion edge at one node | Causal effect of a node change | Lift, p-value/credible interval |
| Difference-in-differences / synthetic control | A market- or region-level node/edge | Causal effect when randomisation is infeasible | ATT (average treatment effect on treated) |
| Uplift model | Individual customer nodes (allocation) | Who to treat, not whether to treat | CATE, Qini/AUUC |
| ICE / RICE | Portfolio of all nodes | Fast ranking / value per effort | Score (ICE, RICE) |
| WSJF / cost of delay | Node value over time | Sequencing to minimise economic CoD | CoD ÷ job size ([T2] SAFe, "WSJF") |
| Theory of constraints | The single constraining node/edge | Throughput of the whole machine | Throughput, buffer health ([T2] Umbrex, "Theory of Constraints") |
| Build–Measure–Learn | Any uncertain transformation | Validated learning at lowest cost | Learning per cycle ([T2] theleanstartup.com, "Build-Measure-Learn") |

The **North Star Metric** framework sits above this table as the alignment substrate: typically acquisition, retention, and monetisation are each defined by a unit of value, a quality filter, and a frequency, then decomposed into a tree of input metrics that specific teams can move ([T2] Reforge, "How to Choose & Measure North Star Metrics," §"Three primary North Star Metrics," ¶1–3). Each node should have one owner accountable for noticing and initiating action on its metric ([T2] Mixpanel, "What is a North Star metric?," §"Metric trees," ¶3). The framework's known failure is sub-optimisation: over-optimising a single number tends to break another, as when LinkedIn chased ad revenue per user and damaged long-term engagement ([T2] North Metric, "North Star Metric Methodology," §"Argued for a constellation," ¶1).

## 3. Hypothesis-Driven Experimentation and the Build–Measure–Learn Cycle

The lean-startup engine is the **Build–Measure–Learn** loop: turn ideas into products, measure how customers respond, and learn whether to pivot or persevere, with the ordering inverted so that learning starts first — identify what you need to learn, then what to measure, then the smallest thing to build ([T2] theleanstartup.com/principles, "Build-Measure-Learn"; [T3] StartupBooks.com, "The Lean Startup," §"The order of the loop"). Ries's unit of progress is **validated learning**, confirmed by real customer behaviour rather than surveys or founder conviction, and the **MVP** is defined by the ratio of learning to effort, not by feature count ([T2] Eric Ries, "What Is an MVP?," §"definition," ¶1). In practice the loop only works when each phase is time-boxed: a two-to-three-day build of one testable change, a five-to-seven-day measurement period, and a one-day decision to keep, kill, or iterate, with the change isolated so attribution is possible ([T3] Felix Lenhard, https://felixlenhard.com/blog/the-build-measure-learn-loop-in-practice/). The "minimum" is genuinely ambiguous, and practitioners resolve it by going with the smallest possible increment and learning from it ([T2] Kent Beck, "Minimum Viable Product revisited," §"MVP," ¶2).

The quantitative workhorse is the **A/B/n test**, the gold standard for causation because randomisation makes the treatment the cause of metric differences with known probability ([T1] Kohavi & Chen 2024, "False Positives in A/B Tests," KDD '24, Abstract ¶1). But the base rates are sobering: most experiments fail to improve key metrics, with success rates of only about 10–20%, so the industry-standard α = 0.05 implies a high probability of false positives ([T1] Kohavi & Chen 2024, Abstract ¶2). A study of over 2,100 real-world tests across an experimentation vendor's customer base found a ~40% false-positive rate, and Shopify's growth team found that of short-term significant improvements, 35% disappeared over a twelve-month horizon ([T2] Jason Cohen, "p-Hacking your A/B tests," §"Marketers: The accidental p-hackers," ¶1–3).

The recurring pitfalls cluster into a few classes. **Power and sample size**: without an estimate of the minimum detectable effect, teams cannot know when to stop, and underpowered tests produce false negatives and unstable positives ([T2] PostHog, "A/B testing mistakes I learned the hard way," §"Ending tests too early," ¶3). **Peeking**: stopping on first significance inflates false positives and biases the effect estimate upward; the fixes are a fixed sample size, group-sequential designs, or fully sequential always-valid inference — the last of which is robust but requires roughly two to seven times more data ([T1] Nordin & Schultzberg 2024, arXiv:2405.03487, §"Introduction," ¶4 and §"Comparison," ¶4). Spotify's **fixed-power design** offers a middle path: start without a predefined sample size, estimate the required size from in-experiment variance, and stop when reached, which permits standard non-sequential inference ([T2] Spotify Engineering, "Fixed-Power Designs," §"TL;DR," ¶1). **Segmentation and Simpson's paradox**: an aggregate winner can be a loser in every subgroup, so results must be broken down by device, geography, tenure, and channel ([T2] PostHog, §"Only viewing results in aggregate," ¶2; [T3] Hacker News thread 41180356, comments). **Guardrails**: counter-metrics (retention, session duration, DAU) and post-experiment holdouts catch side-effects a conversion win can hide ([T2] PostHog, §"Neglecting counter metrics," ¶1–2). **Data dredging**: using the same data to generate and validate a hypothesis is p-hacking; slices need pre-registered hypotheses and fresh data to confirm ([T3] Hacker News thread 41180356, comments; [T3] thread 41679891, comments). At the program level, the empirical guidance from 27 practitioners across 12 companies is to build processes and infrastructure gradually, combine quantitative and qualitative methods, and be mindful of goal alignment and ethics ([T1] Ros, Runeson & Bjarnason 2024, "Evidence-Based Guidelines for Advancing Continuous Experimentation," IT Professional, Abstract ¶1).

Two further systems issues matter when experiments are treated as a portfolio. **Metric choice**: north-star metrics such as long-term revenue or retention are delayed and insensitive, making experiments long and false negatives common; learnt short-term proxy metrics can raise statistical power substantially and cut required sample size ([T1] Jeunen & Ustimenko 2024, "Learning Metrics that Maximise Power for Accelerated A/B-Tests," KDD '24, Abstract ¶1–2). **Speed vs. truth**: adaptive traffic allocation (multi-armed bandits, Bayesian batch algorithms) can identify winners faster but risks false-positive inflation when arms are equivalent, so trustworthiness must be evaluated alongside efficiency ([T1] Zhang & Yuan 2024, "Practical Batch Bayesian Sampling Algorithms," WWW '24, Abstract ¶1–2). In production, bandits add state-management and interference complexity that often outweighs their gain unless feedback is immediate ([T3] Hacker News thread 42686807, comments).

## 4. Causal Inference for Interventions That Cannot Be Randomised

Many interventions — a price change, a market launch, a policy response — cannot be randomised at the unit level. Here the toolkit shifts to quasi-experimental methods. The **difference-in-differences (DID)** estimator compares the change in a treated unit with the change in controls, relying on a parallel-trends assumption that is frequently violated when assignment is non-random; matching or forward selection of control subsets can reduce this risk ([T1] Li 2024, "Frontiers: A Simple Forward Difference-in-Differences Method," Marketing Science 43(2), Abstract ¶1; [T2] AMA, "Causal Inference with Quasi-Experimental Data," §"DID," ¶1–2).

**Synthetic control (SC)** generalises DID by constructing a weighted average of control units whose pre-treatment trajectory matches the treated unit, then attributing post-treatment divergence to the intervention; it is described by Athey and Imbens as "arguably the most important innovation in the evaluation literature in the last fifteen years" ([T2] AMA, §"Synthetic Control," ¶1). Its pretrends assumption is testable only informally by visual inspection, and inference historically relied on placebo tests with restrictive assumptions, addressed by subsampling-based inference theory ([T1] Li & Shankar 2023, "A Two-Step Synthetic Control Approach," Management Science, Abstract ¶1; [T2] AMA, §"Synthetic Control," ¶2). Two refinements matter for business use: **synthetic DID** reweights both units and time to weaken parallel-trends reliance and is invariant to additive unit and time shocks ([T1] Arkhangelsky et al. 2021, "Synthetic Difference in Differences," §"Abstract," ¶1); and **forward/augmented DID** handles short pre-treatment windows where SC needs roughly ten or more pre-periods ([T2] AMA, §"Flexible alternatives," ¶3). A retail application using SC with Lasso regression to build control groups from competitor stores estimated a 3.7% revenue lift from an assortment change, validated with placebo studies ([T1] VC, Prakash & PM 2024, "Measuring the impact of assortment change…," JBRMR, Abstract ¶1). A practical warning from this literature is that if the pretrends assumption fails, do not adopt the method at all ([T2] AMA, §"Best practices," ¶1).

**Uplift modelling** moves from "does the treatment work?" to "who should receive it?" by estimating the Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE) and targeting only **persuadables** — not **sure things** (would buy anyway), **lost causes** (won't respond), or **sleeping dogs** (would churn if contacted) ([T2] Perpetual docs, "Customer Retention: Uplift Modeling," §"Customer churn," ¶1; [T1] Teinemaa, Albert & Goldenberg 2023, "Uplift Modeling: from Causal Inference to Personalization," arXiv:2308.09066, §"Introduction," ¶1). Because treatment has a cost, the real problem is a constrained allocation — often framed as a multiple-choice knapsack or min-flow problem — maximising incremental outcome subject to budget ([T1] Teinemaa et al. 2023, §"Constrained uplift," ¶1; [T1] "End-to-End Cost-Effective Incentive Recommendation under Budget Constraint," RecSys '24, §"Introduction," ¶3). Evaluated with uplift curves, Qini, and AUUC, uplift targeting improved retention while touching only 6.5% of a population relative to a propensity-only policy ([T1] "Segment Discovery: Enhancing E-commerce Targeting," arXiv:2409.13847, §"Case study," ¶2). The sober caveat is that uplift's vaunted advantage over plain predictive targeting has often failed to materialise in benchmarks and live campaigns, especially under class imbalance and small effects — classical churn prediction sometimes beats uplift ([T1] Verhelst 2023, "A churn prediction dataset… benchmark for uplift modeling," arXiv:2312.07206, §"Conclusion," ¶1; [T1] Verhelst PhD thesis, §"Abstract," ¶3).

Finally, the machine's connectivity breaks the stable-unit-treatment-value assumption. In marketplaces, individual-level randomisation can overstate total effects by up to 100% because treatment spills between buyers and sellers; a meta-experiment on Airbnb found that at least ~20% of the naive total average treatment effect was interference bias, eliminated by **cluster randomisation** ([T1] Holtz et al. 2024, "Reducing Interference Bias in Online Marketplace Experiments Using Cluster Randomization," Management Science, Abstract ¶1). Cluster randomisation reduces power, and its effectiveness depends on cluster quality ([T1] Holtz et al. 2024, §"Discussion," ¶1). Bipartite estimators (exposure-reweighted linear and covariate-adjusted variants) extend this to two-sided marketplaces where treatment is assigned to one side and outcomes measured on the other ([T1] "Measuring Sell Side Outcomes… using In-Experiment Bipartite Graph," arXiv:2409.04174, §"Introduction," ¶2–3; [T1] Shankar et al. 2024, "A/B testing under Interference with Partial Network Information," PMLR v238, Abstract ¶1).

## 5. Prioritising and Sequencing an Initiative Portfolio

Once candidate interventions exist, they must be ranked and sequenced. **ICE** (Impact × Confidence × Ease, 1–10 each) was popularised by Sean Ellis for growth teams to triage many experiment ideas quickly, with no reach data required ([T3] Janek Behrens, "RICE vs. WSJF vs. ICE," §"ICE scoring," ¶1; [T2] Tempo, "9 product prioritization frameworks," §"ICE," ¶1). **RICE** (Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort), developed by Sean McBride at Intercom, adds a reach term measured in people or events per fixed period, uses a 3/2/1/0.5/0.25 impact scale, a 100/80/50% confidence scale, and effort in person-months, explicitly to curb bias toward pet ideas and to divide attention across audiences ([T2] Intercom, "RICE: Simple prioritization for product managers," §"RICE: Four factors," ¶1–2). RICE does not account for time sensitivity or dependencies, so its score is an input, not a verdict ([T2] Tempo, §"RICE," ¶2; [T3] Behrens, §"RICE struggles when," ¶1).

Time is handled by **Weighted Shortest Job First (WSJF)**, the SAFe sequencing model: Cost of Delay divided by job duration, where Cost of Delay = user-business value + time criticality + risk reduction/opportunity enablement ([T2] Scaled Agile Framework, "WSJF," §"Definition," ¶1). Cost of Delay itself, attributed to Don Reinertsen and Joshua Arnold, converts the cost of waiting into dollars per unit time so that a feature losing $30k/week outranks one losing $10k/week at equal build time ([T2] Tempo, §"Cost of delay," ¶2). The choice is contextual: ICE when ideas serve roughly the same audience and a fast relative order is enough; RICE when reach varies materially and data exists; WSJF at the portfolio or epic level where deadlines and shared capacity dominate ([T3] Behrens, §"Decision matrix," ¶1; [T3] fygurs.com, "RICE vs WSJF vs ICE vs MoSCoW," §"portfolios," ¶1).

**Theory of Constraints (TOC)** supplies the sequencing logic at the level of the machine itself. Goldratt's five focusing steps — identify the constraint, exploit it, subordinate everything else to it, elevate it, then repeat as the constraint migrates — locate the single node or *policy* that caps throughput and direct all effort there ([T2] Umbrex, "Theory of Constraints," §"Key ideas," ¶1; [T2] Sap, "Theory of Constraints," §"Drum-Buffer-Rope," ¶1). Its measurement lens, **throughput accounting**, reclassifies inventory as money tied up (not an asset to be maximised) and prioritises increasing Throughput, then reducing Investment, then cutting Operating Expense — in that order ([T2] Umbrex, §"Throughput Accounting," ¶1–4). Drum-Buffer-Rope protects the constraint with a buffer and paces release with a rope so upstream non-constraints do not flood work-in-process ([T2] SAP, §"Drum-Buffer-Rope," ¶1–3). Applied to a business graph, a growth constraint might be a conversion node (a "lubricant" problem), a capacity node (elevation), or a policy in the prioritisation process itself; empirical supply-chain work reports efficiency gains when the thinking processes are adopted alongside DBR ([T1] "Influence of Theory of Constraints on Optimizing Supply Chain Management in the USA," IJRISS 2024, §"Findings," ¶3).

## 6. Tracking the Portfolio Against Constraints and Benchmarks

A portfolio of initiatives is only coherent if it is measured against the constraints and benchmarks defined at the financial and engine layers. The first discipline is to track **cohort-level** economics rather than monthly aggregates, because volume growth can mask deterioration in unit economics — a company can post 20% aggregate revenue growth while every new acquisition cohort is less profitable than the last, a Simpson's-paradox effect that aggregate P&Ls hide for 12–18 months ([T3] productphilosophy.com, https://productphilosophy.com/articles/cohort-based-unit-economics). CAC payback — the months until a cohort's cumulative gross profit equals its acquisition cost — is the most operationally useful single metric because it measures how long capital is trapped, and a lengthening payback raises the working capital required to sustain growth ([T3] productphilosophy.com, https://productphilosophy.com/articles/cohort-based-unit-economics; [T2] Lenny's Newsletter, "How to describe your business as an equation," §"Payback," ¶1).

The second discipline is to bind each layer's benchmark to the constraint that binds the machine. Cashflow constrains the paid and sales loops through payback and LTV:CAC; the legal/regulatory layer constrains pricing, advertising, and data use; brand and mission/values bound which experiments are permissible and how far short-term metrics may be pushed. Guardrail and counter-metrics operationalise these bounds — a conversion win that degrades retention or trust is a loss ([T2] PostHog, "A/B testing mistakes," §"Neglecting counter metrics," ¶2). Because objectives are partly qualitative, continuous-experimentation guidance stresses combining quantitative and qualitative evidence and attending to ethics and incentive design ([T1] Ros et al. 2024, Abstract ¶1).

The emerging agentic layer does not change this logic; it accelerates it. Agent-built A/B testing runs the whole loop — hypothesis, real-code variant, deploy, monitor, analyse, learn — over an MCP interface, with human review before launch ([T2] pagent.ai, "The AI A/B testing agent," §"Agent-native," ¶1–2). The AutoResearch loop formalises the cycle as observe → hypothesise → create → deploy → monitor → analyse → learn, with a structured experiment log that feeds hypothesis generation so 20–30 cycles surface segment-level effects a human would miss ([T3] MindStudio, "How to Build a Self-Improving A/B Testing Agent," §"AutoResearch loop," ¶1–2). Simulation can pre-screen variants with persona-driven LLM agents before live traffic, and offline policy evaluation can substitute logged data for live tests, though automation introduces its own failure mode — *algorithmic mode collapse*, where a code-editing research loop keeps making semantically identical edits and its in-loop metric diverges from held-out performance, requiring diversity-aware sampling and a validation gate ([T1] Lu et al. 2025, "AgentA/B," §"Simulation-Supported Design," ¶1; [T1] Wu et al. 2025, "GrowthHacker," TOSEM, Abstract ¶1; [T1] "Beneath the Diff: Diagnosing and Mitigating Algorithmic Mode Collapse…," §"Abstract," ¶1). The governance implication is unchanged: an agentic harness needs the same fixed sample sizes, guardrails, holdouts, and constraint checks as a human team — plus explicit protection against optimising the measurable proxy instead of the underlying business value. The machine can be optimised faster, but only against a scoreboard that still resolves to the P&L, the balance sheet, and the non-negotiable constraints.
