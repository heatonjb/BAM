# Existing Ontologies of a Business as a System

## What a unified "business machine" graph would have to contain

A business-as-machine model must treat the firm as a transformation graph: a stock of assets and liabilities, a set of events and activities that convert inputs into sellable outputs, actors and roles that perform those conversions, and a constraint envelope (cash, legal, contractual, mission, values, brand) that bounds admissible transformations. Five testable criteria recur when judging the formalisms below:

1. **Accountability of resources and events** — can the model record economic inflows/outflows and their duality (every acquisition is someone's disposal)?
2. **Flow and transformation structure** — can it express inputs being consumed to produce outputs, with quantities and rates?
3. **Actor and role assignment** — can it bind behaviour to responsible agents?
4. **Constraint and rule expressiveness** — can it carry business rules, goals, policies and obligations?
5. **Machine-readability and tooling** — is there a serialization plus maintained software, not just a drawing convention?

No single established standard satisfies all five. The useful material is distributed across accounting ontologies, enterprise-architecture languages, value-delivery models, financial-data standards, and knowledge-graph platforms.

## Accounting and economic-flow ontologies: REA, ISO 15944-4, e3value, reference ontologies

**REA (Resources–Events–Agents)** is the closest existing thing to an accounting-native transformation graph. It is "an elementary set of concepts derived from basic definitions in accounting and economics," illustrated by the simple Resource–Event–Agent class structure that gives it its name [T2] ISO/IEC 15944-4 Preview, "Introduction", p. 1. Resources are objects of economic value, events are the transfers that change them, and agents are the persons or organizations that participate. REA originated in accounting information systems and "gradually developed to cover all areas where value modeling can be utilized" [T1] Hunka & Zacek 2014, LNBIP 174, p. 61, ¶1. Its central virtue is **duality**: every increment event (acquire, produce) pairs with a decrement event (use, sell, pay), which gives the model a built-in double-entry consistency that generic process diagrams lack.

REA is formally standardised as the accounting and economic ontology inside **ISO/IEC 15944-4**, the Open-edi Business Transaction Ontology (OeBTO). The 2015 edition (second edition, reviewed and confirmed in 2021) provides UML class diagrams, a business-transaction state machine, and an internal constraint component that is "its repository for business rules" [T2] ISO/IEC 15944-4:2015, Abstract. It applies to binary buyer–seller collaborations and mediated third-party collaborations [T2] ISO/IEC 15944-4:2015, Abstract. The standard explicitly traces its own theoretical background to REA and to UN/CEFACT standards work [T2] ISO/IEC 15944-4:2007, Abstract. REA has also been used in ebXML and the UN/CEFACT modelling methodology [T2] ISO/IEC 15944-4:2015 Preview, "Introduction".

Its weakness is decisive for an agentic harness: the standard itself states that "a formal, machine-readable specification is not proposed in this part of ISO/IEC 15944," even while adopting Gruber's definition of an ontology as a *formal* (machine-readable) specification [T2] ISO/IEC 15944-4:2015 Preview, §2, "formal = machine-readable". In other words REA supplies the right **semantics** for resources, events and agents, plus a state machine and a rule repository, but its default artefact is UML diagrams rather than an operational knowledge graph. A second, well-documented failure is adoption: accounting data models from REA research "are not incorporated in ERP systems" [T1] Vandenbosche & Wortmann 2006, as cited in Hunka & Zacek 2014, references. REA also says little about non-exchange constraints (mission, values, brand) and nothing about the financial statement layer — it models transactions, not the balance sheet roll-up.

**e3value** complements REA at the network level. It is an ontology-based methodology for modelling business networks, built on "the principle of reciprocity emphasizing the dual character of business transactions" [T1] Gordijn & Akkermans, "A UML Profile for the e3-Value e-Business Model Ontology," BUSITAL'08, p. 1. Its concepts are actor, value object, value port, value interface, value activity, value exchange, plus dependency and AND/OR connections [T1] Gordijn, Osterwalder & Pigneur 2005, "Comparing Two Business Model Ontologies," §4. It is semi-formal — "sufficient formality to reason about business rules and expected profitability" — and can attach valuation functions to exchanges [T1] Gordijn, Osterwalder & Pigneur 2005, §4. Weigand's evaluation notes that the approach "is based on an explicit ontology and supported by a graphical tool," and proposes "value cycle" soundness as a model-quality feature [T1] Weigand 2016, *Journal of Information Systems* 30(2), pp. 113–133, Abstract. e3value's economic granularity (who gives what of value to whom) is excellent, but it is deliberately *not* a process model: it tracks transfer of ownership/rights rather than physical possession or internal activities, and the two views do not align automatically [T1] "Guidelines to derive an e³value business model from a BPMN process model," *Software and Systems Modeling* (2023), §1.

The most directly reusable bridge is the **reference ontology** of Andersson et al., which proposes one business-model ontology "using concepts from three established business model ontologies; the REA, BMO, and e3-value" [T1] Andersson et al. 2006, *ER 2006*, LNCS 4215, pp. 482–496, Abstract. Later work confirms such a reference ontology "allows model transformations between Resource-Event-Agent (REA), e3-value, and BMO" [T1] Iacob & Nieuwenhuis, "A Meta-Model Perspective on Business Models," §4. This is the single strongest prior-art candidate for composition, but it is a 2006 academic artefact, not a maintained toolchain.

## Enterprise-architecture and process languages: ArchiMate, BPMN, TOGAF

**ArchiMate** (The Open Group) is the most complete general-purpose enterprise graph language. Version 3.2 is the official specification, published 28 November 2022, and defines an open, independent modelling language for enterprise architecture supported by multiple tool vendors [T2] The Open Group, ArchiMate 3.2 Specification (C226), "Abstract". It provides a generic metamodel plus relational semantics and a layered framework: a Motivation layer (stakeholder, driver, assessment, goal, outcome, requirement, **constraint**, principle, meaning, value), a Strategy layer (capability, resource, course of action, value stream), and core Business, Application and Technology layers, plus an Implementation & Migration layer [T2] The Open Group, ArchiMate 3.2 Reference Cards (N221), "Motivation Elements," "Strategy Layer Elements," "Business Layer Elements". The Business layer covers actors, roles, collaborations, processes, functions, interactions, events, services, objects, contracts, representations and products [T2] ArchiMate 3.2 Reference Cards, "Business Layer Elements". Crucially, ArchiMate has a **standard Model Exchange File Format** for moving models between tools, so it is machine-tractable in practice, not merely visual [T2] The Open Group, ArchiMate Model Exchange File Format, https://www.opengroup.org/xsd/archimate/3.0.

ArchiMate's strength is coverage of *goals and constraints alongside structure and behaviour* — the only one of the mainstream languages with a first-class motivation layer. Its weakness is that this breadth is shallow on economics: it models capabilities and value streams but not quantities, cash or double-entry duality. Formal analysis work also found that "ArchiMate does not provide a straightforward mapping to [BWW] State element," and that neither BPMN 2.0 nor ArchiMate 2.0 "has the ability to describe lawful states and events of the systems at the different abstraction levels" [T1] "Linking BPMN, ArchiMate, and BWW," CEUR-WS Vol-1023, §3–4. In other words, ArchiMate is a rich typed graph but not an executable state machine.

**BPMN** (OMG) is the de-facto executable process notation. Version 2.0 (January 2011; 2.0.2 December 2013) added, unlike 1.x, a formal metamodel, execution semantics, XSD interchange and a machine-readable representation, and it was later published by ISO as ISO/IEC 19510:2013 [T2] OMG, BPMN 2.0.2 (formal/2013-12-09), "Exchange Formats," and OMG BPMN spec page. Section 13 defines "execution semantics for orchestrations" that give "a clear and precise understanding of the operation of executable BPMN elements," and the spec maps BPMN to WS-BPEL [T2] OMG BPMN 2.0, §13 and §14. Open-source engines such as jBPM implement a "Common Executable" subset with events, activities and gateways connected by sequence flows, and exchange XML that tools consume directly [T2] jBPM 5.3 User Guide, "Core Engine: BPMN 2.0". BPMN is thus the best available **flow/transformation executor**, but it is process-scoped: it "is specifically designed for modeling business processes in detail" and is "not suitable for enterprise-wide architecture," so resources, actors and economics live outside the model [T3] ArchiMetric, "Comprehensive Guide: ArchiMate vs. BPMN vs. UML," https://www.archimetric.com/comprehensive-guide-archimate-vs-bpmn-vs-uml. Reconciling BPMN with ArchiMate is an active mapping problem because "BPMN and ArchiMate models are standards with different abstraction levels, therefore gaps exist between these two standards" [T1] CEUR-WS Vol-1023, §1.

**TOGAF**, by contrast, is *not* a graph language at all — it is a method. The 10th Edition (launched 25 April 2022) is "a detailed method and a set of supporting tools" centred on the iterative Architecture Development Method (ADM), reorganised into six Fundamental Content documents plus TOGAF Series Guides [T2] The Open Group, "The Open Group Announces Launch of the TOGAF Standard, 10th Edition," and "The TOGAF Standard, 10th Edition" (C220). Its Architecture Content document "describes the TOGAF Content Framework and a structured metamodel for architectural artifacts," and it ships Series Guides for Business Models, Business Capabilities, Value Streams, Information Mapping and Organization Mapping [T2] The Open Group, C220, "The TOGAF Standard — Architecture Content"; "TOGAF Series Guides" [T3] Detecon, "The TOGAF Standard, 10th Edition — What's new?". TOGAF therefore contributes a **process for governing change** and a catalogue of viewpoints, not a queryable model. Empirical critique is blunt: in case companies that nominally "used" TOGAF, "ADM — the core of TOGAF — is found inapplicable in practice and its steps are not followed by any of these companies," and the Architecture Content Framework and Enterprise Continuum "are also not used" [T3] Kotusev, "Enterprise architecture is not TOGAF," BCS, https://www.bcs.org/articles-opinion-and-research/enterprise-architecture-is-not-togaf. For a business-machine harness, TOGAF is useful as a governance wrapper, not as a data model.

## Value-delivery and business-model mental models: VDML, BMC/BMO

**VDML** (Value Delivery Modeling Language, OMG) is the most ambitious attempt to be the missing middle layer. Version 1.1 (adopted March 2018; document formal/18-09-03) defines "a standard modeling language for analysis and design of the operation of an enterprise with particular focus on the creation and exchange of value," and ships an XMI machine-consumable file and MOF/MDA-compliant metamodel [T2] OMG, VDML 1.1, Preface and "Normative Machine Readable Documents". Its metamodel explicitly "has been developed to support value chain, value stream and capability analysis, and has been refined to support Value Network Analysis (VNA), e³-value modeling, REA (Resource Event Agent) analysis and an owner/investor business model," and it "supports modeling and analysis of both tangible and intangible business value" and is "compatible with resource management frameworks utilizing intangible assets as well as traditional financial assets" [T2] OMG, VDML 1.1, §1.1. Operationally, capabilities are supported by resources and methods, activities require and engage capabilities, and analysis runs over "networks of activities and deliverable flows"; a scenario is a set of measurements attached to model elements [T2] OMG, "VDML: Modeling for Managers," and Sparx Systems EA User Guide, "VDML Language Overview". It even provides "libraries of re-usable model elements … to enforce the use of standardized business vocabulary."

VDML absorbs seven prior value models — Porter's Value Chain, Osterwalder's Business Model Ontology, Value Network Analysis, e3value, REA, Value Stream Mapping and Service-Oriented Business Architecture [T3] Wikipedia, "Value Delivery Modelling Language," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_Delivery_Modelling_Language. That composability is exactly what a business-machine graph needs. The caveats: the abstraction is deliberately managerial rather than transactional (it models "statistical characteristics of activities, resources, deliverables and value contributions" [T2] Sparx Systems, "VDML Language Overview"), it overlaps semantically with BPMN and ArchiMate without a single canonical mapping [T2] Sparx Systems, "VDML Language Overview"; [T1] "The Value Management Platform and ArchiMate – Towards an Integration?", CEUR-WS Vol-2574, §1, and tooling/maintenance momentum has been limited (mostly VDMbee's Value Management Platform). VDML is best treated as the **reuse skeleton** for a value/capability layer.

**The Business Model Canvas / Business Model Ontology (BMO)** is the popular, lightweight end of the spectrum. Osterwalder's original BMO is a genuine academic ontology with nine interrelated building blocks, Value Proposition first [T1] Osterwalder 2004, *The Business Model Ontology — A Proposition in a Design Science Approach*, PhD dissertation, University of Lausanne, Abstract. Its authors treated it as semi-formal reasoning material: the goal of formalising it "with a description logic system" was to "check the consistency and satisfiability of the business model" and to enable "automatic comparison of business models" [T1] Osterwalder, Parent & Pigneur 2004, "Setting up an ontology of business models," CEUR-WS Vol-125, §1 and §3. A later comparison records that BMO "has evolved from a structured informal ontology to a semi-formal one that is described in OWL" [T1] Gordijn, Osterwalder & Pigneur 2005, §4. The 2010 *Business Model Generation* book turned the ontology into the familiar sticky-note canvas [T2] Osterwalder & Pigneur 2010, *Business Model Generation*.

As a machine graph the canvas breaks comprehensively. Reviews find it excludes external forces such as competition and market factors, narrows the value proposition to a revenue-return framing, and leaves the mechanisms *between* blocks undefined [T1] Coes 2014, "Critically Assessing the Strengths and Limitations of the Business Model Canvas," University of Twente, Abstract. It "does not include an analysis of the competition," was "intended to be used by entrepreneurs focused on profit generation," "does not incorporate external forces," and "lacks a formalized process for analyzing the data collected" [T1] Sibalija et al. 2021, *SAGE*, "Limitations". It over-simplifies complex/multi-sided models, is static and rigid, and gives only "shallow financial insights … potentially ignoring deeper financial considerations, such as cash flow management, investment needs, and risk planning" [T1] "Business Model Canvas Application in Start-up Stage Business Developments," *ERsJ* 2024, "Results". One comparative study states it has "no connection of components into causal chain that would demonstrate the connectivity and bonds of elements" and "does not allow the clear practical application to concrete numerical results" [T1] "Analysis of Business Models," *Journal of Competitiveness*, §3. Practitioner critiques add that its "static nature becomes particularly problematic in fast-moving industries" and that profitability timing, seasonality and risk are absent [T3] Shah Mohammed, "The Business Model Canvas: A Critical Analysis of Its Fundamental Flaws," Medium, https://shahmm.medium.com/the-business-model-canvas-a-critical-analysis-of-its-fundamental-flaws-a702021959a7. The canvas is therefore a **vocabulary checklist** worth reusing for node labelling, but not a model.

## Financial-statement and instrument ontologies: XBRL and FIBO

A business-machine graph anchored on a P&L and balance sheet needs a formal representation of the statements themselves, and this is where the strongest machine-readable standards already exist.

**XBRL** (eXtensible Business Reporting Language) is "a markup language for standardized coding of business reports," XML-based, maintained by XBRL International, and explicitly "machine-readable and can therefore be processed automatically" [T2] XBRL CH Taxonomy 2025, Guidance, p. 3. It is organised as taxonomies — "dictionaries for reporting" [T2] XBRL International, "Taxonomies," https://www.xbrl.org/the-standard/what/key-concepts-in-xbrl/taxonomies/ — whose groups map cleanly onto the statements we need: Statement of Financial Position (balance sheet), Statement of Income, Statement of Cash Flows, Statement of Shareholder Equity [T2] FASB, 2025 US GAAP Financial Reporting Taxonomy Technical Guide, and XBRL CH Taxonomy 2025, Fig. 2. Taxonomies carry "calculation relationships" enforcing rules such as a + b = c, dimensional structures, and additional validation rules that software can enforce, so reports that violate the rules can be flagged automatically [T2] XBRL International, "Taxonomies". The limitation is that extension taxonomies let companies invent terms, so "where companies have disclosed information which might be comparable in different ways, XBRL can't provide a 'magic bullet'" [T2] XBRL International, "Financial Statements in XBRL". XBRL gives the graph its **statement layer and comparability spine**, but it only describes reported aggregates — it does not tell you which operationally-transformed asset produced them.

**FIBO** (Financial Industry Business Ontology, EDM Council, standardised by OMG) supplies the instrument and contract layer in full OWL/description logic. It is "a global, standardized ontology … designed to represent financial concepts, terms, and their relationships in a precise, machine-readable way," organised into modular domains with concepts, relationships and attributes, and it is "built on knowledge graph technology" [T2] EDM Council, "FIBO," https://edmcouncil.org/frameworks/industry-models/fibo. Its Foundations module is "a modularized formal model of the concepts represented by finance industry terms as used in official financial organization documents such as contracts, product/service specifications and governance and regulatory compliance documents," covering non-financial primitives such as contracts, commitments, transactions, organizations and ownership [T2] OMG, FIBO Foundations v1.1, §1 and §2. It is formal enough that OWL reasoners can operate over it, and production releases are published quarterly after hygiene tests [T2] EDM Council, "FIBO OWL". FIBO's scope is finance, not the whole firm: it can type an asset or liability precisely, but it will not model a marketing funnel or a production line.

## Digital-twin and knowledge-graph stacks

Modern enterprise platforms have independently converged on the graph shape this project wants. Palantir Foundry describes its Ontology as "the digital twin of an organization, integrating the organization's digital assets (datasets and models) into a coherent whole," built from object types (entities/events), properties, link types (relationships) and action types (mutations), plus functions and interfaces [T2] Palantir, "Ontology: Core concepts," https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/ontology/core-concepts. This is effectively a generic business-machine schema with mutation semantics — but it is proprietary and its semantics are organisation-specific rather than standardised. Microsoft's Azure Digital Twins similarly uses DTDL models and industry "ontologies" to define a twin graph [T2] Microsoft Learn, "What is an ontology? (Azure Digital Twins)". Vendor analyses frame the combination of knowledge graphs and digital twins as "connective tissue" that yields a 360° view of assets, service levels and customer experience [T3] Ontotext, "Digital Twins and Knowledge Graphs," https://www.ontotext.com/blog/digital-twins-and-knowledge-graphs. The academic grounding is real but young: a 2024 systematic literature review finds that "knowledge graphs, acting as interconnected and interrelated databases, provide a means of seamlessly integrating different data sources, facilitating the efficient integration and automated adaption of data and (simulation) models in the Digital Twin" [T1] Listl et al. 2024, "Knowledge Graphs in the Digital Twin," IEEE, Abstract. OpenText's maturity model distinguishes asset-, system-, process- and enterprise-level twins, where the enterprise-level twin "links system-level twins to deliver a view across the entire enterprise" and its goal is "to monitor and optimize higher-level business activities and outcomes" [T2] OpenText, "The ever-evolving digital twin," White Paper. These stacks provide the **substrate and query engine** for a business machine, but they ship no business semantics — you must supply the ontology, and the value they add is integration, simulation and action, not theory.

## Where each breaks as a unified business-machine graph

| Formalism | Represents well | Formality / machine-readability | Resources / flows / actors / constraints | Tooling & maintenance | Breakpoint |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| REA / ISO 15944-4 | Economic duality of exchanges; transaction state machine | UML semantics; ISO standard, but "machine-readable specification is not proposed" [T2] | R/E/A excellent; internal flows and non-economic constraints weak | McCarthy research corpus; limited ERP adoption [T1] | No operational serialization; no statement layer; no mission/brand |
| e3value | Value exchange in multi-actor networks; profitability reasoning | Semi-formal, graphical tool, valuation functions | Value objects/exchanges excellent; physical flows weak; actors good | Maintained by The Value Engineers | Ownership-centric, not a process/transform engine |
| ArchiMate 3.2 | Goals, constraints, capabilities, layered structure | Formal metamodel + standard exchange file format | Structure/actors/constraints strong; quantities, cash, duality absent | The Open Group, active, many tools | Not an executable state machine; no economics |
| BPMN 2.0 | Executable transformation and control flow | Formal metamodel + execution semantics + XML; ISO 19510 | Flow excellent; resources/actors via extensions; economics/constraints absent | OMG + many engines (jBPM, Camunda) | Process-scoped; not enterprise- or statement-aware |
| TOGAF 10 | Governance method, viewpoints, change process | Prose method + content metamodel; not executable | Abstract coverage; no quantities | The Open Group, active; ADM often ignored [T3] | Method, not a queryable model |
| VDML 1.1 | Value, capability, activity and deliverable flows; intangible value | MOF/MDA metamodel + XMI | Close to full coverage; financial detail statistical | OMG + mainly VDMbee | Managerial abstraction; weak momentum; overlaps ArchiMate/BPMN |
| BMC/BMO | Vocabulary of business-model blocks | BMO semi-formal/OWL in principle; canvas informal | Blocks only; no causal links, no numerics | Book/community, not a maintained toolchain | No mechanics, no financials, static, ignores competition [T1] |
| XBRL | Statements and their calculation rules | XML, machine-readable, validated | Financial aggregates only | XBRL International + FASB, very active | Reported totals only; no operational causation |
| FIBO | Financial instruments, contracts, entities, ownership | OWL/description logic, reasoner-ready | Instruments/contracts excellent; no processes | EDM Council + OMG, active | Finance-domain only |
| Digital-twin / KG stacks | Integration, simulation, action over a typed graph | Formal object/link/action schemas (proprietary or DTDL) | Generic substrate; semantics are user-supplied | Palantir, Microsoft, industrial vendors | No standard business ontology; proprietary lock-in |

## Composition: what to reuse and what must be built new

The prior art decomposes cleanly into reusable layers rather than a single winner. A defensible composition:

- **Transaction and duality semantics** — reuse REA's Resource–Event–Agent pattern and its ISO/IEC 15944-4 state machine and business-rule repository as the accounting primitives [T2] ISO/IEC 15944-4:2015. This is the natural home for the "transform asset into another asset" edges.
- **Value-network and actor semantics** — reuse e3value's actor/value-object/value-exchange concepts and the Andersson et al. reference ontology to reconcile them with REA and BMO [T1] Andersson et al. 2006; [T1] Gordijn & Akkermans, BUSITAL'08.
- **Structure, capability, goal and constraint semantics** — reuse ArchiMate's Motivation/Strategy/Business layers and its Model Exchange File Format [T2] The Open Group, ArchiMate 3.2. This supplies the constraint envelope (goal, requirement, constraint, principle, value) that REA and XBRL lack.
- **Execution and transformation flow** — reuse BPMN 2.0 execution semantics for the operational sub-graph [T2] OMG BPMN 2.0.
- **Financial statement and instrument typing** — reuse XBRL taxonomies for statement line items and their calculation rules, and FIBO for instruments, contracts, parties and ownership [T2] FASB GAAP Taxonomy; [T2] OMG FIBO Foundations. XBRL supplies the P&L/balance-sheet anchor; FIBO supplies the asset/liability types.
- **Value and capability abstraction with scenarios** — reuse VDML's activity/deliverable-flow network and measurement/scenario mechanism as the middle layer [T2] OMG VDML 1.1.
- **Substrate** — reuse a knowledge-graph/digital-twin object–property–link–action schema (Palantir-style or RDF/OWL) for storage, simulation and action [T2] Palantir Foundry Ontology; [T1] Listl et al. 2024.

What must be **built new** is the connective tissue and the evaluative layer:

1. **A single upper ontology and identity/alignment map.** The reference ontology of Andersson et al. is the nearest precedent but is academic and unmaintained; there is no production-grade, maintained OWL alignment joining REA, ArchiMate, BPMN, VDML, XBRL and FIBO. This is the core deliverable.
2. **Quantitative transformation algebra.** None of the formalisms natively couples a stock of resources to flow rates, yields, unit economics and cash-conversion timing. Stock-and-flow and activity-based-costing semantics have to be added on top of the REA/BPMN skeleton.
3. **The constraint envelope beyond ArchiMate's motivation layer.** Mission, values, brand and legal/temporal constraints need operationalisable semantics (rules, caps, invariants) rather than architectural boxes, so they can gate experiments an autonomous agent proposes.
4. **A benchmark/peer layer.** XBRL and FIBO give machine-readable company facts, but comparative benchmarking "at each level" of the graph (unit economics, capability, flow) requires a cross-company metric ontology that does not yet exist.
5. **A live instance graph.** All the standards are *meta*-models or *reporting* schemas. Turning a specific firm's ledgers, CRM, ERP and telemetry into a maintained instance of the composed model — with an action/mutation API for agents — is engineering that currently exists only inside proprietary platforms.

In short: REA/ISO 15944-4, e3value, ArchiMate, BPMN, VDML, XBRL and FIBO between them already cover meaningfully most nodes and edges a business-machine graph needs, and the Andersson reference ontology proves the top-level concepts can be merged. The gap is not conceptual coverage but (a) a maintained, machine-readable alignment across them, (b) quantitative stock-and-flow and economic semantics, (c) operationalised constraint rules, and (d) an instance-level graph with agent-callable actions.
