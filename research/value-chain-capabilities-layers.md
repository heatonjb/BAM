# Value Chain, Capabilities, and Layers of Abstraction

## 1. The problem: one business graph, many altitudes

A business can be modelled as a single connected graph whose nodes are assets, liabilities, agents, and transformation steps, and whose edges are flows of resources, information, and commitments. The central representational difficulty is not drawing that graph but choosing the right **altitude** at which to reason about any part of it. A CFO reasons at the altitude of the financial statements; an operations manager at the altitude of a process; a software team at the altitude of a transaction. Each altitude uses a different vocabulary, a different unit of analysis, and a different set of metrics, yet all describe the same underlying machine. The sections below survey the canonical frameworks that impose layers of abstraction over a business and then propose a concrete stacked-layer scheme.

The recurring design pattern across frameworks is the separation of **what a business can do** (capabilities, resources, stocks) from **how it does it** (activities, processes, events), and the anchoring of both to **why** (strategy, mission, objectives). Porter's value chain splits activities into primary and support [T2] Porter, "The Value Chain," HBS Institute for Strategy and Competitiveness, §"The Value Chain"; the resource-based view and dynamic capabilities foreground the resource base and the routines that reconfigure it [T1] Teece, Pisano & Shuen 1997, abstract, ¶1; and business capability modelling sits explicitly between strategy and execution [T2] LeanIX, "Business Capability Modeling Guidelines," §"Overview of all relations to/from business capability". Enterprise-architecture languages such as ArchiMate formalise the cross-layer links as typed relationships — serving, realization, assignment, triggering — that a machine can traverse [T2] ArchiMate 3.0 Specification, §3.3 "Layering of the ArchiMate Language". The Business Model Canvas offers a complementary flat, nine-block cross-layer template (segments, value proposition, channels, activities, resources, partners, costs, revenues) rather than a strict hierarchy [T3] Wikipedia, "Business model canvas," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_Model_Canvas. Beer's Viable System Model instead makes recursion the organising principle: each operational unit is itself a viable system with its own S1–S5, so "any viable system contains, and is contained in, a viable system" [T3] bisks.net, "viable," https://viable.bisks.net/.

## 2. Porter's value chain and the value system

Porter's value chain disaggregates a company into "strategically relevant activities in order to focus on the sources of competitive advantage, that is, the specific activities that result in higher prices or lower costs" [T2] Porter, "The Value Chain," HBS ISC, §"The Value Chain". The firm is decomposed into five **primary activities** — inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service — and four **support activities**: procurement, technology development, human resource management, and firm infrastructure [T2] Recklies 2001, "The Value Chain," p. 1, primary/support classification. Critically, linkages between activities — not the activities in isolation — are "crucial for corporate success," and most firms are elements of a larger **value system** spanning upstream suppliers and downstream channels [T2] Recklies 2001, p. 2, ¶1; [T2] Porter, HBS ISC, §"The Value Chain". Strategy, in this frame, is "the set of choices about how the activities in the value chain are configured and linked together" [T2] Porter, HBS ISC, §"set of choices".

The value chain's contribution to a layered model is a **generic, firm-specific template of activity categories** and the assertion that competitive advantage lives in *linkages* — edges, not nodes. Its limitation is that the categories are coarse and manufacturing-era, and it does not natively connect to stocks, liabilities, or cash.

## 3. Resources, ordinary capabilities, and dynamic capabilities

The resource-based view (RBV) holds that "it is stocks that are at the core of sustainable competitive advantage" and defines a capability as the firm-specific efficiency with which inputs are converted to outputs — "the capacity to utilize resources to perform a task or an activity" [T1] Collis & Anand 2018, p. 2, §"A) Resources and Capabilities". Dynamic capabilities are the higher-order routines that change the resource base. A canonical definition is "the firm's ability to integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external competences to address rapidly changing environments" [T1] Teece, Pisano & Shuen 1997, abstract, ¶1; [T2] Teece, "Dynamic Capabilities," davidjteece.com, ¶1. Dynamic capabilities are explicitly distinguished from **operational** ("ordinary") capabilities, which "pertain to the current operations of an organization"; dynamic capabilities are "the capacity of an organization to purposefully create, extend, or modify its resource base" [T2] Teece, "Dynamic Capabilities," ¶1.

Teece's microfoundations resolve the construct into three skills — **sensing, seizing, and transforming (reconfiguring)** — located in "distinct skills, processes, procedures, organizational structures, decision rules, and disciplines" [T1] Teece 2007, abstract, ¶2; [T2] Teece, "Dynamic Capabilities," §"Processes". This is a crucial structural claim for a layered business model: the *same* firm contains ordinary capabilities operating at the process/activity layer and dynamic capabilities operating at the strategy layer that reconfigure everything below. Teece also introduces **asset orchestration** — "the managerial search, selection, and configuration of resources and capabilities" — and **co-specialisation**, where assets "are uniquely valuable in combination" and therefore more valuable together than in isolation [T2] Teece, "Dynamic Capabilities," §"Asset orchestration" and §"Co-specialization".

The framework's own literature flags a limit that matters for an optimisation harness: pursuing dynamic capabilities trades off against building the lower-level ordinary capabilities needed for static efficiency [T1] Collis & Anand 2018, p. 1, §"Introduction". The three-level structure is summarised as ordinary capabilities that "sustain current operations" versus dynamic capabilities that "transform and renew" them [T1] "Dynamic capabilities framework and its transformative contributions," JIBS 2024, §"Capabilities", yielding a two-tier capability hierarchy.

## 4. Capability maps, value streams, and process hierarchies

Business capability modelling formalises the "what a business can do" layer. The Business Architecture Body of Knowledge (BIZBOK) treats capabilities as "the fundamental functions, activities, and abilities that are required for an enterprise to thrive" and presents the capability map as the "commonly used business blueprint" [T2] Ulrich & Rosen, "The Business Capability Map," Cutter Consortium, §"The Business Capability Map". Capabilities **decompose into more capabilities**, with tiering that distinguishes "strategic (1), core or customer facing (2) and supporting (3)" capabilities [T2] OMG, "Business Architecture Reference Model Workshop," §"A REVIEW OF CAPABILITY MAP BASICS". A capability is nonetheless not a process or value stream: anything requiring "movement… some sequence of activities" veers into *how* something is done and belongs to a process category [T2] BPMInstitute, "Defining the Business Capability – A Cheat Sheet," §"Capabilities are unique in terms of intent".

Value streams provide the orthogonal, end-to-end view. A value stream is "an end-to-end collection of activities that create a result for a customer," represented as progressing stages in verb/noun form such as "Process Payment," and it "should not be confused with business processes because they represent a high-level view that aggregates all paths" [T2] BPMInstitute, "Capabilities & Value Streams," §"The value stream". The mapping rules are explicit and machine-tractable: "capability level three maps to stages within the value stream" and "value stream stage decomposes into business process" [T2] BPMInstitute, "Capabilities & Value Streams," §"Best practice-based mappings".

Below the process layer, the hierarchy runs from the whole value chain down to individual tasks. One canonical six-level **Processing System Hierarchy** starts at the enterprise as a giant processing system, descends to the value chain ("the means by which the organization creates, sells and delivers products and services of value to the marketplace"), then to bundles of Available/Sold/Delivered processes, then to individual end-to-end processes, and finally to sub-processes and single tasks [T2] BPMInstitute, "When You Say 'Process,' You Mean…?," §"Figure 1". BPMN supports the same nesting through sub-processes and tasks, and distinguishes process **maps**, descriptions, and executable models [T2] OMG, "Introduction to BPMN," §"Background" and §"Process Levels". BPMN itself is deliberately scoped to control flow and does *not* model "the relation between organizational structures, including business competencies, capabilities, and resources to processes" [T2] OMG, "The Complete Business Process Handbook," §"BPMN limitations" — which is precisely why a separate capability layer is required.

## 5. Enterprise-architecture and ontological layers

ArchiMate gives the most explicit multi-layer metamodel. The core language defines three layers: the **Business Layer** ("business services… realized by business processes performed by business actors"), the **Application Layer** ("application services that support the business… and the applications that realize them"), and the **Technology Layer** ("technology services such as processing, storage, and communication services") [T2] ArchiMate 3.0 Specification, §3.3 "Layering of the ArchiMate Language". Three extensions supplement these: a **Strategy** layer (resources, capabilities, courses of action), a **Physical** layer (equipment, facilities, materials), and an **Implementation & Migration** layer (work packages, deliverables, plateaus) [T3] Arch-IQ, "ArchiMate Explained," https://www.archiq.app/archimate. Each layer repeats the same internal grammar of active structure, behaviour, and passive structure [T2] ArchiMate 3.0 Specification, §3.4 "Aspects"; [T3] Arch-IQ, https://www.archiq.app/archimate.

What makes ArchiMate directly usable as the backbone of an agentic business graph is its **typed cross-layer relationships**: *serving* ("one element provides a service to another"), *realization* ("a more concrete element fulfils a more abstract one"), *assignment* ("allocation of behaviour to active structure"), *composition/aggregation*, and *triggering/flow* [T3] Arch-IQ, https://www.archiq.app/archimate. The specification notes it does not define a separate information layer; business objects, data objects, and technology objects carry information across layers via the passive-structure aspect [T2] ArchiMate 3.0 Specification, §3.5 "Full Framework".

The accounting ontology **REA (Resources–Events–Agents)** supplies the transaction-layer semantics. REA was introduced as "a generalized accounting framework designed to be used in a shared data environment" and captures three aspects of every exchange: requited events, resources exchanged, and participating agents [T1] McCarthy 1982, abstract, ¶1; [T1] Geerts & McCarthy 2000, §"Exchange". Its core is the **duality** principle: every economic event is paired with a reciprocal event, so the graph is intrinsically flow-and-stock rather than debit/credit [T2] Valueflows, "Core Concepts," §"REA (Resources, Events, Agents)". The ontology was extended "both up (to enterprise value chains) and down" in granularity and along a temporal plane incorporating commitments, contracts, and policies, and it is standardised in ISO/IEC 15944-4 [T1] Geerts & McCarthy 2002, abstract, ¶2; [T2] MSU, "William McCarthy named to Accounting Hall of Fame," ¶6.

The modern implementation **Valueflows** makes REA operational and adds an explicit **three-level ontology**: the *Knowledge* level ("classification, policies, procedures, rules and patterns"), the *Plan* level ("offers and requests, schedules and promises"), and the *Observation* level ("what really happened") [T2] Valueflows, "Core Concepts," §"Levels of the ontology". Valueflows also adds the **Input–Process–Output** pattern, where "one Economic Resource is the output of one Process and then becomes an input to another, thus connecting the processes into a flow," producing "a directed graph of value flows" that can be traversed forwards (tracking) or backwards (tracing) [T2] Valueflows, "Core Concepts," §"IPO" and §"Directed graphs of flows".

At the transaction layer, **process mining** demonstrates that the graph is discoverable from raw event logs. Process mining "assumes the existence of an event log where each event refers to a case, an activity, and a point in time," and its discovery techniques construct process models directly from observed behaviour [T1] van der Aalst 2012, ACM TMIS 3(2), Article 7, §"Introduction"; [T2] processmining.org, "Event Data," §"Data for process mining". Modern automated process discovery is benchmarked across twelve public and twelve proprietary real-life event logs [T1] Augusto et al. 2019, IEEE TKDE 31(4), abstract, ¶1. This is the empirical bridge that lets a model infer the activity and process layers from transaction-level data rather than hand-drawn diagrams.

## 6. The strategy-to-execution ladder

Frameworks that cascade intent downward converge on a similar ladder. **Hoshin Kanri** (policy deployment) proceeds from a clear vision and current-state assessment, to breakthrough objectives, annual objectives, cascading, execution, and review [T2] Lean Enterprise Institute, "Hoshin Kanri," §"process," ¶1; [T3] HoshinCloud, "Hoshin Kanri 101," https://www.hoshinonline.com/hoshin-kanri-methodology-strategy-execution. It aligns "functions and activities to strategic objectives vertically and horizontally" and treats capability development as part of deployment [T2] Lean Enterprise Institute, "Hoshin Kanri," §"What's more". The **Balanced Scorecard** provides the causal chain that makes the ladder directional rather than merely hierarchical: objectives are linked in cause-and-effect relationships from employees through processes and customers to higher financial performance, and the **strategy map** visualises those links [T1] Kaplan & Norton, "Conceptual Foundations of the Balanced Scorecard," HBS working paper 10-074, §"Strategy Maps," ¶2; [T2] HBS Online, "What Is a Balanced Scorecard?," §"How to Create a Balanced Scorecard". The four perspectives — learning and growth, internal process, customer, financial — map cleanly onto the capability/process/customer/financial layers [T2] HBS Online, §"The four perspectives include".

Modern goal-cascade frameworks formalise the same chain as an explicit four-level dependency: a company priority → team objective → key results → initiatives, where the distinction between outcome (key result) and activity (initiative) is deliberately preserved "so you can change tactics without changing the goal" [T3] OKRstool, "Strategy Execution Framework," https://www.okrstool.com/blog/strategy-execution-framework. The V2MOM (Vision, Values, Methods, Measures, Obstacles) and the product-management "Vision Stack" order the ladder as Vision → Objectives → Strategy → Roadmap → Plans, with strategy defined as "how best to achieve those objectives" [T3] Saeed Khan, "Strategy and the Vision Stack," https://swkhan.medium.com/strategy-and-the-vision-stack-9948bb1a6ae5; [T2] The Strategy Institute, "V2MOM Framework Explained," §"Methods". The consensus ladder is therefore **vision/mission → strategy (choices) → objectives (outcomes, measurable) → capabilities → activities/initiatives → transactions**, with feedback loops running back up through reviews.

## 7. A concrete stacked-layer scheme

Synthesising the frameworks above yields a seven-layer stack over one business graph. Each layer is a projection: nodes at layer *n+1* aggregate nodes at layer *n*, and edges across layers are typed according to the ArchiMate and REA vocabularies.

| # | Layer | Node type | Canonical source | Representative metrics |
|---|-------|-----------|------------------|------------------------|
| 0 | Transactions / events | Economic event, journal entry, API call | REA/ValueFlows Observation level | unit price, quantity, event cost, event cycle time, timestamp |
| 1 | Activities | Atomic transformation / BPMN task | ABC activity; Porter activity categories | activity cost-driver rate, unit time (TDABC), throughput per activity |
| 2 | Processes / value streams | End-to-end sequence, value-stream stage | BPMN; value-stream stage; TOC constraint | lead time, cycle time, first-pass yield, WIP, process cost, throughput |
| 3 | Capabilities | "What the firm can do" (ordinary + dynamic) | Capability map / BIZBOK; Teece sensing–seizing–transforming | maturity, coverage/gap, cost-to-serve, change velocity |
| 4 | Assets / liabilities | Resource stock: physical, human, IP, financial, co-specialised | RBV resource base; balance sheet | book value, asset utilisation, capacity, co-specialisation |
| 5 | Financial statements | P&L, balance sheet, cash-flow aggregates | REA-derived views / XBRL taxonomy | revenue, gross margin, EBITDA, ROE/ROIC, FCF |
| 6 | Strategy | Vision, objectives, choices, policies | Hoshin Kanri; Balanced Scorecard strategy map | market share, NRR, TAM penetration, strategic-objective attainment |

**Edges that cross layers.** The stack is only useful if the inter-layer edges are typed, because those are the levers an experiment can pull.

- **Transaction → Activity** (attribution): transactions are aggregated into activities via resource drivers; activity-based costing "assigns overhead to products based on the various activities that drive overhead costs" and computes activity rates from cost pools [T2] OpenStax, *Principles of Managerial Accounting*, §6.3. Time-driven ABC refines this by estimating "the unit cost of supplying capacity" and "the consumption of capacity (unit times) by the activities" [T1] Kaplan & Anderson 2004, HBS working paper 04-045, §"The essence of activity-based costing," ¶1.
- **Activity → Process** (composition/sequence): activities compose into processes via ordering and triggering relations, exactly as BPMN sub-processes nest within processes [T2] OMG, "Introduction to BPMN," §"A Sub-Process is a compound activity".
- **Process → Capability** (realization): a capability is "realized by a combination of business process, people and technology," which is what places it above the process layer [T2] LeanIX, "Business Capability Modeling Guidelines," §"Overview of all relations". In ArchiMate terms this is the *realization* relationship, "a more concrete element fulfils a more abstract one" [T3] Arch-IQ, https://www.archiq.app/archimate.
- **Capability → Asset** (assignment/requires): capabilities require and are bounded by the resource base; capabilities are "framed by the roles and resources that have those capabilities" [T2] BPMInstitute, "Defining the Business Capability – A Cheat Sheet," §"Capabilities can be framed by the roles and resources". Teece's asset orchestration is the dynamic-capability edge that reconstitutes this subgraph [T2] Teece, "Dynamic Capabilities," §"Asset orchestration".
- **Asset → Financial statement** (valuation/recognition): stocks and flows are valued and recognised into accrual statements; Valueflows shows that "accounting views use the Dependent or Individual Agent view" and that "accounting statements can also be updated instantly for each participant in any economic event as soon as the event is recorded" [T2] Valueflows, "Accounting," §"Recording Basic Economic Activity".
- **Financial → Strategy** (target-setting): objectives are expressed against financial and non-financial measures, and the Balanced Scorecard builds the causal chain from employee learning and process outcomes to financial outcomes [T1] Kaplan & Norton, HBS working paper 10-074, §"Strategy Maps," ¶2. In Hoshin Kanri this edge is the deployment of breakthrough objectives into annual objectives [T2] Lean Enterprise Institute, "Hoshin Kanri," §"process".

Two cross-cutting properties emerge. First, **the same node can be viewed at multiple layers** — a single sales transaction is simultaneously a REA economic event, an activity instance in a value stream, evidence of a sales capability, and a revenue line-item. Second, **cross-layer ratio metrics are the natural optimisation targets**: unit economics such as CAC (allocating sales-and-marketing spend to channels and periods) and LTV (combining ARPA, gross margin, and cohort retention) span activities/processes and financial statements [T3] The SaaS CFO, "CAC Payback Period vs LTV/CAC," https://www.thesaascfo.com/saas-metrics-showdown-cac-payback-period-vs-ltv-cac; [T3] Stripe, "CAC SaaS," https://stripe.com/resources/more/cac-in-saas. TDABC spans activities and capacity (assets); DuPont decomposes ROE into "net profit margin, asset turnover, and equity multiplier" [T3] Wikipedia, "DuPont analysis," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DuPont_analysis; and throughput accounting replaces cost allocation with throughput, inventory, and operating expense as the primary measures [T1] "Throughput Accounting," SAGE Encyclopedia of Healthcare Management, §"Throughput Accounting". Theory of constraints adds a *system-level* rule — the five focusing steps (identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate, repeat) locate the binding constraint at whichever layer it occurs and subordinate all other layers to it [T2] TOC Institute, "Five Focusing Steps," §"The Five Focusing Steps"; [T2] ACCA, "Throughput accounting," §"The five focusing steps".

## 8. Benchmarking at each layer and the agentic implication

Benchmarking must happen at the layer being optimised, because metrics are not comparable across layers. At the **capability and process** layers, software-delivery research offers a rare cross-company benchmark: DORA's five metrics split into throughput (change lead time, deployment frequency, recovery time) and instability (change fail rate, rework rate), reported as the *outcomes* of an underlying capability catalogue [T2] DORA, "DORA's software delivery performance metrics," §"Throughput" and §"Instability". Team Topologies encodes the corresponding organisational rule — optimise for "fast flow, rapid feedback and limits on team cognitive load" [T3] Thoughtworks, "Team topologies and effective software delivery," https://www.thoughtworks.com/en-us/insights/podcasts/technology-podcasts/team-topologies.

At the **financial and asset** layers, standardised machine-readable reporting makes peer benchmarking tractable: the FASB US GAAP Financial Reporting Taxonomy provides the element vocabulary (prefixes `us-gaap-` and `srt-`) for tagging financial statements, and XBRL US publishes the annual releases [T2] FASB, *GAAP Financial Reporting Taxonomy Technical Guide 2026*, §"Prefixes for the Main File Groups"; [T2] XBRL US, "2025 US GAAP Financial, SEC Reporting and DQC Rules Taxonomies," §"US GAAP Financial Reporting Taxonomy". At the **transaction and activity** layers, process-mining event logs and ABC cost-driver rates are comparable within a firm and increasingly across firms in the same vertical [T1] Augusto et al. 2019, abstract, ¶1; [T1] Kaplan & Anderson 2004, §"Activity Cost Driver Rate".

The practical implication for an agentic business-strategy advisor is that an experiment is always an intervention on a specific edge or node at a specific layer, and its effect propagates along the typed cross-layer edges: a pricing experiment acts at the transaction layer but manifests at the financial layer, while a capability investment reshapes processes through realization and assignment edges. The modelling work is therefore twofold: (1) instantiate the seven-layer stack as a typed graph — using ArchiMate's relationship grammar for the business/application/technology cross-links and REA/ValueFlows for the transaction-and-accounting substrate — and (2) attach layer-appropriate metrics plus the cross-layer ratio metrics, so each candidate initiative can be scored against a consistent, comparable objective. No single framework supplies the full stack — Porter the activity-and-linkage view, RBV/dynamic capabilities the stock-and-reconfiguration view, capability modelling the strategy-to-execution bridge, ArchiMate and REA the typed-graph machinery, and the Balanced Scorecard and Hoshin Kanri the causal cascading logic — so a single typed business graph that hosts all of them is the precondition for optimising the business as a machine.
