# How Far Can Mission, Values, and Brand Be Formalised?

A business-machine model must decide, node by node and edge by edge, whether a stated commitment can be compiled into a check the optimiser runs, left as a weighted cost, or escalated to a person. This file tests that limit: how organisations translate mission, values, and brand into measurable behaviours; where that translation collapses into Goodhart-style gaming or performative compliance; what ESG/CSRD digital reporting, brand-safety classification, culture instruments, and deontic policy languages prove about computability; and what the tacit-knowledge and AI-alignment literatures say about the irreducible remainder. It closes with the boundary: which commitments are predicates, which are soft weighted terms, and which must remain human-authorised.

## 1. The mechanical path: values to observable behaviours to scored instruments

The first move every serious framework makes is to strip a value of its adjective and replace it with observable acts. Brené Brown's operationalisation exercise converts "be brave", "serve the work", and "take good care" into behaviour statements ("I give feedback in a respectful and daring way"; "I own my mistakes and discuss them in an open and honest way") each rated on a 5-point Likert scale by employee and manager separately, then reconciled in one-to-one conversations [T2] Brown, *Operationalizing Your Values* (Dare to Lead), pp. 1–2. The Kellogg School's account of Bernie Banks' method is the same four-step loop — articulate, measure against the standard inside performance evaluation, call out behaviour, and run Army-style "climate surveys" that assess whether aggregate behaviour reflects stated values [T2] Kellogg Insight, "Does Your Company Actually Live Its Values?", §"Measure Against Those Standards"–§"Call Out Behavior".

The Culture 500 research program shows a machine-facing version: nine values (agility, collaboration, customer orientation, diversity, execution, innovation, integrity, performance, respect) each with a written construct definition, scored by NLP over employee review text as a sentiment percentage [T2] MIT Sloan Management Review, *Culture 500 — The Big 9 Cultural Values*. The definitions are close to testable predicates — "Employees consistently act in an honest and ethical manner" — but the instrument measures *perceived* expression of a value, not the value itself; that is the crack §5 widens.

The strongest existing example of a values commitment compiled into an auditable standard is B Corp certification. A company must complete the B Impact Assessment, be *verified* to at least 80 of 200 points through documentation review, calls, and possible site visits, publish its score, and — critically — amend its governing documents to require stakeholder rather than shareholder-only governance, recertifying every three years [T2] B Lab, *Certification Guide for Small–Medium Enterprises*, §"Requirements of Becoming a Certified B Corporation". This establishes that mission can be encoded as a versioned, externally-audited, scored standard with a legal lock. But two limits are visible in B Lab's own disclosures: the assessment "is (a) independent of harmonized or international standards and (b) not specifically endorsed or authorized by any government or international authority", and B Lab is "not recognized as a 'conformity assessment body'" [T2] B Lab, *B Corp Certification Legal Notice*, ¶"The BIA uses criteria…". The predicate's authority is private, and its execution still routes through human analysts and legal documents, not automated checks over live business state.

## 2. Goodhartian decay: why the score stops tracking the value

When an organisation pushes on a values metric hard enough to steer behaviour, the metric detaches from the value. The best evidence is a direct empirical test of the espoused/enacted gap. Comparing the official values of more than 500 large organisations against employee perceptions in over 1.2 million Glassdoor reviews, researchers found **no correlation** between the values a company publishes and how well employees say it lives them; all correlations were very weak, four of nine (collaboration, customer orientation, execution, diversity) were negative, and even the best, agility, reached only 0.22 [T1] Sull, Turconi & Sull 2020, *When It Comes to Culture, Does Your Company Walk the Talk?*, MIT Sloan Management Review, §"Data Shows No Correlation Between Official Values and Corporate Culture". A value that is measured but not costly is, on this evidence, nearly uninformative.

Formalisation also backfires at the individual level. Cha and Edmondson show that employee sensemaking triggered by *strong* stated values raises the risk of attributions of leader hypocrisy, producing disenchantment despite good intentions [T1] Cha & Edmondson 2006, "When values backfire: Leadership, attribution, and disenchantment in a values-driven organization", *The Leadership Quarterly* (Cited 545). And when a values-adjacent metric becomes a performance target, the response can be outright gaming: interviews across an academic system identified "gaming performance measurement" — reducing quality while optimising the measured dimension — as a form of deviant workplace behaviour [T1] Graf, Wendler & Stumpf-Wollersheim 2019, *Wanting More, Getting Less: Gaming Performance Measurement as a Form of Deviant Workplace Behavior*, *Journal of Business Ethics* 157, pp. 753–773.

Goodhart's law names the general mechanism: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" [T3] Wikipedia, "Goodhart's law", ¶1, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law. The useful refinement for a business model is the variant taxonomy — regressional, extremal, causal, adversarial, and benchmark Goodhart — which separates failures that more data fixes (regressional noise) from failures no better measurement fixes (the proxy is causally disconnected from the goal; actors deliberately exploit it) [T3] Sustainable Catalyst, "Metrics, Objectives, and Goodhart's Law: When Measures Become Targets", §"Variants of Goodhart Failure". The design response is architectural, not moralistic: guardrail metrics beside primary metrics, equity and process metrics, and a "monitoring metric" that detects drift between proxy and goal [T3] Sustainable Catalyst, §"Governance layer". A values predicate is safe only where it is causally connected to the value and expensive to game.

Notably, values alignment *does* predict performance at a finer grain even though stated values do not: using survey and administrative data from a bank, workers whose personal values diverge from their manager's perform measurably worse, and the productivity loss from maximum misalignment is nearly four times that of a manager of a different gender — a channel the authors attribute to suppressed voice and information-seeking, not demographic difference or misalignment with *organisational* values [T1] Espinosa et al. 2025, *Value Dissonance at Work*, CEPR Discussion Paper DP19929, abstract. The operational lesson is that the measurable thing is relational and local (manager–report value fit), not the corporate value statement.

## 3. ESG and CSRD: the clearest empirical map of what computes

The European Sustainability Reporting Standards and their XBRL transposition are the largest experiment to date in making non-financial commitments machine-readable, and they publish exactly the computability gradient this topic needs. The ESRS XBRL architecture classifies data points by comparability and standalone usability: numerical metrics (GHG emissions, headcount, water), monetary amounts, and percentages rank **high/high**; booleans, enumerations, and dates rank **high/medium**; short unformatted narrative strings rank **medium/low**; and narrative textblocks rank **low/low** [T2] XBRL Europe, *Draft ESRS XBRL Taxonomy: Architecture and Methodology*, §5 "Types of ESRS data points", pp. 8–9. EFRAG published the ESRS Set 1 XBRL Taxonomy in August 2024, providing an XBRL element for every datapoint and dimensional disaggregation and enabling conversion of the sustainability statement into machine-readable form, ultimately to be mandated as part of the ESEF Regulatory Technical Standards [T2] EFRAG, *ESRS Set 1 XBRL Taxonomy — Explanatory Note*, ¶¶18–19, 27. Narrative tagging works by giving one tag per discrete datapoint at the finest level of the human-readable text, deliberately staying "as close as possible to the text of the ESRS" [T2] XBRL Europe, §6 "Narrative tagging", p. 11.

This is a computability partition drawn by a regulator: emissions, headcount, and spend can be predicates; the *quality* of a transition plan cannot, and is carried as text with a tag attached. The recent "slimming" of the standards — mandatory datapoints cut by more than 60% — shows the tension between coverage and operability [T2] XBRL International, "EFRAG advances the revised ESRS toward a digital taxonomy", ¶4. Double materiality itself requires judgement about which sustainability matters are material, which no taxonomy can supply [T1] Wojewódka, *ESG Standards Within CSRD*, Springer, §"Introduction".

Even where the numbers are tagged and comparable, the *aggregation* into a score is unstable. Across six major rating agencies, aggregate ESG rating correlations range from 0.38 to 0.71 (average 0.54); the environmental dimension averages 0.53, social 0.42, and **governance only 0.30** [T1] Berg, Kölbel & Rigobon 2022, "Aggregate Confusion: The Divergence of ESG Ratings", *Review of Finance*, §"Results", ¶¶1–3, DOI 10.1093/rof/rfac033. A separate study of ~1,049 MSCI World firms found average rank correlation 0.58 (range 0.43–0.69) and average *observed agreement of only 24%*, i.e. disagreement larger than one full rating class [T1] Billio et al. 2021, "Inside the ESG ratings: (Dis)agreement and performance", *Corporate Social Responsibility and Environmental Management*, Table 3 and §"Results". Divergence is attributed to differences in scope, measurement, and weighting, and it disperses any effect of ESG on asset prices and makes it hard to tie compensation to ESG performance [T1] Berg et al. 2022, §"Introduction". The formal consequence for a business model is sharp: the *disclosed datapoint* can be a predicate, but the *composite ESG score* is a weighted, rater-specific soft term that should never be treated as an objective.

Where disclosure is unquantified, it drifts toward greenwashing. A review of the non-financial statements of all Global Fortune 500 companies found that 80% did not quantify environmental impacts through life-cycle assessment and almost all of the remaining 20% quantified only a small portion [T1] Ruggeri et al. 2025, "Facing the Risk of Greenwashing in the ESG Report of Global Companies", *Corporate Social Responsibility and Environmental Management*, §"Results". The ECB defines greenwashing as statements that "do not clearly and fairly reflect the underlying sustainability profile" and concedes a hard limitation: when greenwashing is unconscious or involuntary, severe penalties will not correct it [T2] European Central Bank, *Different shades of green: EU corporate disclosure rules and their effectiveness in limiting "greenwashing"*, Occasional Paper 370, §"Introduction" and §"limitations".

## 4. Brand safety: the most machine-checkable values domain — and the most fragile

Brand has two distinct formalisable surfaces, and they behave differently. The first is *brand safety/suitability*: the set of content contexts in which a brand will permit itself to appear. GARM's Brand Safety Floor and Suitability Framework is the clearest case of brand values compiled into predicates. It defines roughly eleven shared content categories and, for each, a **Brand Safety Floor** (content "not appropriate for any advertising support") plus a three-level suitability tier (high/medium/low risk) that advertisers parameterise to their own risk tolerance [T2] GARM, *Brand Safety Floor + Suitability Framework*, §"Brand Suitability Framework". The framework is explicitly designed to be activated by the IAB TechLab's industry-wide taxonomy and adopted by ad-tech providers through "clear mapping or overt integration" [T2] GARM, §"Goals for Solution". The Adjacency Standards Framework adds spatial and temporal units of measure for ad placement [T2] GARM, *Adjacency Standards Framework*, §"Consistent Units of Measure".

This is operational. Integral Ad Science reports a **brand safety rating** as the percentage of ads that passed the GARM floor out of all ads served, and scores content low/medium/high against GARM categories using NLP models trained on the framework; a "99% brand safety rating" means ads did not appear next to floor content 99% of the time [T2] Integral Ad Science, *Brand Safety and Suitability Overview*, §"What is a Brand Safety Rating?". At the category-and-tier level, therefore, brand constraints genuinely are machine-checkable: the Floor is a hard predicate and suitability tiers are ordered soft terms.

Two failures bound the claim. First, the classifiers are imperfect: an Adalytics investigation found hundreds of ads next to explicitly racist and sexual content despite those campaigns using brand-safety controls from Integral Ad Science and DoubleVerify [T3] Campaign US, "GARM's discontinuation 'sad day for industry,' brand safety experts say", ¶"GARM's dissolution comes as the industry loses confidence…". A predicate is only as reliable as its classifier, and content classification is probabilistic. Second, and more fundamentally, the *authority* underpinning the predicate proved contestable: in August 2024, after X filed an antitrust suit alleging that GARM and member brands conspired to withhold ad revenue, the World Federation of Advertisers discontinued GARM, citing drained resources [T3] CNBC, "Advertising group suspends brand safety unit after Elon Musk's antitrust lawsuit", and [T3] Marketing Week, "WFA makes 'difficult decision' to discontinue GARM following X lawsuit". The constraint's *content* was formalised; its *governance* was not durable. A business-machine model that treats industry brand-safety categories as fixed predicates must therefore attach a version and an authority to them, and expect both to move.

The second brand surface is *reputation* — whether the brand promise was kept. RepTrak models this as a "think/feel/do" chain: rational perceptions (products, integrity, treatment of people) drive an emotional bond, and the bond predicts behavioural outcomes (buy, recommend, work for, invest, give benefit of the doubt), resolving to a single normalised 0–100 Reputation Score, built from seven weighted drivers and benchmarked against a global database [T2] RepTrak, "From Vanity Metrics to Decision-Grade Reputation", §"How reputation becomes decision-grade". This is a legitimate measurement asset, but it is a *soft construct of perception*, not a predicate. RepTrak itself documents why the single score hides structure: for an Australian bank, the AI-derived reputation score (43.6) diverged from the informed-general-public score (66.3) by more than 22 points because different audiences weight different drivers [T2] RepTrak, "When You Have A Single Reputation Score, It's Hiding A Lot", ¶2. Brand-values also appear weakly weighted by the market: when people use AI to research companies, they most want prices/fees (22%) and products/services (15%), far ahead of values (8%) or environmental/social impact (5%) [T2] RepTrak, "From Vanity Metrics…", §"The evidence behind the model". Brand promises are therefore best modelled as *constraints on admissible actions* (predicates) plus a *reputation-perception term* (weighted), not as a single crisp objective.

## 5. Culture measurement and the tacit floor

The culture-measurement industry has the deepest psychometric validation of any values instrument, and its own literature concedes the ceiling. The Denison Organizational Culture Survey is a 60-item, 12-index, 4-trait instrument (Involvement, Consistency, Adaptability, Mission) with index reliabilities of .70–.86, validated against 35,474 individuals across 160 organisations and benchmarked to a global normative database of over 1,000 organisations, with demonstrated criterion-related validity against sales growth, market share, profitability, and customer satisfaction [T1] Denison & Neale 1996, *Denison Organizational Culture Survey*, APA PsycTests, DOI 10.1037/t64206-000; [T2] Denison Consulting, *The Denison Organizational Culture Survey* overview, §"Overview". The Organisational Culture Assessment Instrument, built on the Competing Values Framework, has been re-validated for factor structure and criterion validity on 328 employees [T1] Chatman et al. 2014, "Validation of the Organizational Culture Assessment Instrument", *PLOS ONE* 9(3), abstract. A review of the field nonetheless describes the construct as "elusive" and notes that organisational culture research relies on qualitative case methods tracking how values are transmitted through myths and stories [T1] Hsieh, Lange, Rodin & Wolf-Bauwens, "Getting clear on corporate culture", *Journal of the British Academy* 6(s1), p. 157.

The reason no instrument closes the gap is structural, and Schein's three-level model states it best. Culture comprises visible **artifacts**, **espoused beliefs and values** (the strategy/goals/philosophy layer, i.e. the values statement), and **basic underlying assumptions** — "unconscious, taken-for-granted beliefs and values" that "truly drive behavior" and are "the hardest to change" [T2] Schein, *Organizational Culture and Leadership*, §"The Three Levels of Organizational Culture". The diagnostic move is precisely to compare espoused values against artifacts until an inconsistency reveals the deeper assumption: a firm that espouses teamwork but rewards individuals has revealed an individual-accountability assumption beneath the slogan [T2] Schein, *The Corporate Culture Survival Guide*, §"Compare Values with Artifacts". Espoused values, Schein notes, "often leave large areas of behavior unexplained" [T2] Oregon ODA, *Understanding Culture is Key to Company Success* (Schein summary), §"Basic Underlying Assumptions".

This is where Polanyi supplies the philosophical floor. Tacit knowing is knowledge "we can know more than we can tell", and Polanyi's stronger claim is that explicit knowledge "must rely on being tacitly understood and applied. Hence all knowledge is either tacit or rooted in tacit knowledge. A wholly explicit knowledge is unthinkable" [T1] Polanyi 1966, *The Tacit Dimension*, p. 7 (Univ. of Chicago Press). Rules of art, he holds, "do not determine the practice of an art… They cannot replace this knowledge" [T1] Polanyi 1958/2002, *Personal Knowledge*, p. 50, as quoted in *Tacit Knowledge: Making It Explicit*, LSE FACTS report, p. 2. Contemporary work reaches the same conclusion: tacit knowledge is rooted in action and shared context, and whether it can be codified remains unresolved [T1] Piore 2025, *Tacit Knowledge and the Future of Work Debate*, MIT, pp. 1–7. For a business model this means the deepest driver of "how we actually behave" cannot in principle be reduced to a complete predicate set; it can only be approximated, monitored for drift, and periodically re-read by humans.

## 6. Escalation as a first-class control: deontic policy and human oversight

The right response to the tacit floor is not to abandon formalisation but to formalise the *escalation* — to make "this class of decision must be human-authorised" itself a machine-checkable rule. Three mature bodies of work provide the machinery.

First, normative multi-agent systems formalise exactly the vocabulary a values model needs. Rea/Caerostar and deontic logic represent **obligations, permissions, and prohibitions** as first-class norms, and the normative-MAS literature treats norms as a domain-independent theory plus mechanisms for detecting and resolving normative conflict [T1] Boella, van der Torre et al. 2007, *Introduction to Normative Multiagent Systems*, Dagstuhl Seminar Proceedings 07122, §3. A crucial limit is documented: Standard Deontic Logic cannot represent contrary-to-duty scenarios (what must be done *after* a primary obligation is violated) consistently, which is why richer logics are needed [T1] Braga, Haeusler & Santos 2017, *Modeling Normative Multi-Agent Systems from a Kelsenian Perspective*, arXiv:1709.02018, abstract. This is the formal counterpart of the human-judgement problem: real values conflicts are contrary-to-duty situations, and they are the ones that resist clean predicate encoding. Newer work applies deontic policies to govern agentic AI at runtime, expressing permissions and prohibitions in OWL/RDF and evaluating them with a logic engine *outside* the LLM, with explicit meta-policies for resolving overlapping authority between organisational, project, and regulatory rules [T1] *Deontic Policies for Runtime Governance of Agentic AI Systems*, arXiv:2606.19464, §"Methods". In industry practice the same idea is realised as policy-as-code: Open Policy Agent evaluates declarative Rego policies over in-memory data and returns decision outcomes with audit trails, deployed as a decision point close to the action [T2] Open Policy Agent, *Documentation* and *How to Deploy OPA*.

Second, agent runtimes now treat human approval as an explicit control type rather than a fallback. OpenAI's agents guide distinguishes automatic guardrails (input, output, tool) from human-in-the-loop approvals that "pause the run so a person or policy can approve or reject a sensitive action", and recommends pausing "before side effects like cancellations, edits, shell commands, or sensitive MCP actions" [T2] OpenAI, *Guardrails and human review*, §"Choose the right control" and §"Pause for human review". Declarative guardrail languages encode graded effects — allow, deny, require human approval, AI-in-the-loop review, or content-safety filter — selected by condition [T2] Agent Policy Spec, *guard* README, §"Effects".

Third, and most binding, the EU AI Act makes human oversight a legal requirement for high-risk systems: they "shall be designed and developed in such a way… that they can be effectively overseen by natural persons", oversight must counteract automation bias, enable the operator to "decide… not to use… or to otherwise disregard, override or reverse the output", and provide a stop mechanism; for remote biometric identification, no action may be taken unless "separately verified and confirmed by at least two natural persons" [T2] EU AI Act Service Desk, *Article 14: Human oversight*, ¶¶1–4 and ¶5; [T2] artificialintelligenceact.eu, *Article 14* (in force 2 Dec 2027 for Annex III systems). This is the strongest public statement that a class of decisions must not be delegated to a machine, regardless of how well it scores.

The AI-alignment literature explains why oversight cannot be fully replaced. Optimising a learned proxy for a value reproduces Goodhart's law inside the model: reward hacking or "reward overoptimization" is where a policy "exploits imperfections in the learned proxy… achieving high rewards while deviating from true human objectives", and larger reward models do not eliminate it [T1] Miao et al. 2025, *Information-Theoretic Reward Modeling for Stable RLHF*, arXiv:2510.13694, abstract; [T1] Hu et al. 2025, *Reward Hacking in Reinforcement Learning and RLHF*, IEEE, abstract. Constitutional AI reduces this by having a model self-critique against a written "constitution" of principles, but the principles are themselves natural language, and the trade-off between helpfulness and harmlessness persists [T1] Bai et al. 2022, *Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback*, arXiv:2212.08073, pp. 1–2. LLM alignment is also culturally and context dependent, and current methods "often yield superficial conformity rather than genuine ethical understanding" [T1] *Diverse Human Value Alignment for Large Language Models via Ethical Reasoning*, arXiv:2511.00379, abstract; empirical work finds "value forks" — systematic differences in moral judgement by agent type — that resist any single ground-truth label [T1] *The Hard Problem of AI Alignment: Value Forks in Moral Judgment*, FAccT '25, pp. 2671–2681. The design implication is consistent: use LLM judgements and classifiers as *soft scorers or advisory layers*, keep hard predicates deterministic, and reserve the contested cases for humans.

## 7. The boundary: predicates, weighted terms, human-authorised

The evidence supports a three-tier partition, and the line between tiers is set by two tests: **(a) is the underlying quantity causally connected to the value, and (b) can a classifier or rule evaluate it deterministically and cheaply?**

**Tier 1 — machine-checkable predicates (hard or guardrail).** Commitments whose object is an enumerable category, a numeric threshold, or a contractual/legal term. Evidence: ESRS numeric/monetary/percentage datapoints are high-comparability and separable [T2] XBRL Europe §5; GARM's Brand Safety Floor is a categorical hard exclusion and suitability tiers are ordered classes [T2] GARM; policy-as-code returns deterministic allow/deny/HITL decisions with audit trails [T2] OPA; B Corp's eligibility, score threshold, and legal requirement are verifiable conditions [T2] B Lab. Predicates are safe where they are near the *floor* (what is forbidden) rather than the *aspiration* (how good we wish to be).

**Tier 2 — weighted soft terms.** Commitments that must be scored, traded off, and monitored for drift: brand-suitability risk tolerance, composite ESG ratings, culture index scores, reputation scores, and mission-alignment scores of proposed initiatives. Evidence: ESG composite correlations as low as 0.30–0.71 across raters make a single score rater-dependent [T1] Berg et al. 2022; reputation scores diverge sharply by audience [T2] RepTrak; culture instruments are validated but measure perception and must be triangulated [T1] Denison & Neale; [T1] Hsieh et al. These should enter the objective as penalties or guardrail bands, not as vetoes, and must carry an explicit "monitoring metric" that watches proxy–goal drift [T3] Sustainable Catalyst.

**Tier 3 — human-authorised.** Decisions where the value is tacit, contested, contrary-to-duty, or where honouring it imposes a real cost that only accountable people may choose: materiality determinations, trade-offs between conflicting values, novel cases that no precedent covers, brand-defining or irreversible moves, and post-violation remediation. Evidence: Schein's basic assumptions drive behaviour beneath espoused values and cannot be fully articulated [T2] Schein; Polanyi's claim that "a wholly explicit knowledge is unthinkable" [T1] Polanyi 1966, p. 7; the inconsistency of Standard Deontic Logic on contrary-to-duty obligations [T1] Braga et al. 2017; the persistence of reward hacking and value forks in AI systems [T1] Miao et al. 2025; [T1] FAccT '25; and, decisively, the EU AI Act's Article 14 mandate that high-risk systems be effectively overseen by natural persons who can override or halt them [T2] EU AI Act Art. 14.

The dividing line, stated as a rule for the harness: **compile to a predicate what is enumerable, causally valid, and cheap to check; keep as a weighted term what must be balanced against other goals and watched for gaming; and route to a human what is tacit, contested, or costly to honour — and make that routing itself an explicit, audited guardrail.** The symmetric mistake to avoid: over-formalising values manufactures Goodhartian gaming and performative compliance, while refusing to formalise leaves the optimiser free to violate the commitments the business exists to keep.
