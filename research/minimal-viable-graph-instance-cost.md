# Minimal viable graph and the cost of instantiating a live business graph

## Minimal viable graph schema

A minimal viable graph (MVG) for a business-as-transformation machine must reproduce the financial statements as projections and support concrete optimisation decisions without encoding every operational artefact. The core constraint is parsimony: capture only what causally drives P&L, balance sheet, and cash flows [T1] (Geerts & McCarthy, 2000, pp. 157-159, ¶2; McCarthy, 1982, p. 554, ¶1). For this purpose, the Resources-Events-Agents (REA) ontology provides a minimal economic core that links resources, events and agents via duality and stock-flow relations [T1] (McCarthy, 1982, pp. 554-557, ¶2-¶5; Geerts & McCarthy, 2000, pp. 158-160, ¶2-¶3). REA’s focus on economic exchange and conversion events maps directly to the transformation view of a business (inputs transformed into sellable outputs) while remaining agnostic to process minutiae [T1] (McCarthy, 1982, p. 554, ¶1; Geerts & McCarthy, 2000, pp. 157-159, ¶2). 

The fewest node types required are: **Resources** (assets/liabilities/cash), **Economic Events** (exchanges, conversions), **Agents** (internal/external parties), **Products/Services** (sellable resource types), **Customers/Channels**, **Capabilities** (minimal transformation capacity), and **Contracts/Obligations** (commitments that create future cash obligations/liabilities) [T1] (McCarthy, 1982, pp. 554-556, ¶2-¶4; Laurier et al., 2018, pp. 211-212, ¶1-¶2). Critically, “commitments” and “contracts” are not decorative — they bridge policy-level constraints (recurring revenue terms, payment terms, debt covenants) to operational events [T1] (McCarthy, 1982, pp. 556-557, ¶4; Geerts & McCarthy, 2000, pp. 165-167, ¶3). To remain consistent with accounting, the schema must track **duality** (each increment requires a corresponding decrement/event pair) and enforce that event values reconcile to the resources they affect [T1] (McCarthy, 1982, pp. 555-556, ¶3-¶4; Hruby, 2006, Ch. 4, Sec. 4.3, line 14-28) [T2]. This is the bedrock for generating P&L, balance sheet and cash flow from event-level data rather than from period-end artefacts [T1] (Geerts & McCarthy, 2000, pp. 158-160, ¶2-¶3).

The edge types are correspondingly minimal: **stock-flow** (resource <-> event), **participation** (agent <-> event), **duality** (event <-> event), **custody/ownership** (agent -> resource), **contractual obligation** (contract -> obligation -> event), and **transformation** (capability/resource inputs -> event -> resource outputs) [T1] (McCarthy, 1982, pp. 554-557, ¶2-¶5; Laurier et al., 2018, pp. 211-213, ¶2-¶3). These are sufficient to model exchanges (revenue/AR/payments) and conversions (materials/labour/capacity -> finished goods/COGS) — the two activities that drive most unit economics [T1] (McCarthy, 1982, pp. 556-557, ¶4-¶5; Hruby, 2006, Ch. 4, Sec. 4.2, line 7-21) [T2]. 

At the parameter level, an MVG needs only driver-level quantities: unit prices, production coefficients (input per output), cost rates for capacity resources (labour/equipment), payment terms, collection delays, inventory turns, fulfilment cycle times, and capacity constraints [T1] (McCarthy, 1982, pp. 556-557, ¶4; Laurier et al., 2018, pp. 213-214, ¶3). These are exactly the parameters that feed DuPont/driver-based planning and unit economics (CAC, LTV, contribution margin, payback) [T2] (Horngren et al., 2012, Ch. 5, Sec. 5.1, line 5-19; Kaplan & Anderson, 2007, Ch. 1, Sec. 1.2, line 10-25). A concrete optimisation — price or spend experiment — requires only: product/customer nodes, conversion inputs with coefficients, revenue/cost rates, and capacity/working-capital constraints; from these one can project impact on gross margin, contribution, cash runway, and simple payback [T2] (Kaplan & Anderson, 2007, Ch. 2, Sec. 2.2, line 8-22; Horngren et al., 2012, Ch. 11, Sec. 11.2, line 3-16). Adding more granularity (every task, screen, document) rarely improves first-order price/spend decisions, but omitting duality/stock-flows breaks reconciliation to financial statements [T1] (Geerts & McCarthy, 2000, pp. 158-160, ¶2-¶3; McCarthy, 1982, pp. 555-556, ¶3). 

In short: MVG = (Resources, EconomicEvents, Agents, Products/Services, Customers/Channels, Capabilities, Contracts) + minimal edges + driver parameters. This is sufficient to reproduce three-statement projections and support a concrete price/spend optimisation without full process graph fidelity [T1][T2].

## How graphs are actually built (ERP/CRM, ETL/ELT/dbt, event sourcing, XBRL)

Realising an MVG as a live instance means materialising it from operational systems. The typical source of truth for economic events/resources is the ERP (payables/receivables, inventory, revenue recognition, chart of accounts), supplemented by CRM (customers/contracts) and billing systems [T2] (ISO/IEC 15944-4, 2007, Sec. 4.2, line 4-12; Geerts & Wang, 2007, pp. 167-170, ¶2-¶3). Integrations are the bottleneck: mapping disparate ledgers to a unified event model (REA/EO) requires explicit schema mapping, often using a canonical intermediate schema [T2] (Geerts & Wang, 2007, pp. 168-171, ¶3-¶4).

Modern practice favours ELT/ETL into a warehouse/lakehouse with transformation layer (dbt-style) to build a “semantic/finance layer” that normalises transactions into a dimensional/event model [T2] (Kreps, 2014, Sec. “Modern Data Pipeline”, line 2-15; Armbrust et al., 2021, pp. 28-31, ¶2-¶3). Event sourcing (recording state changes as immutable events) aligns naturally with REA events and duality, and helps reconstruct history for projections and audit [T2] (Fowler, 2005, “Event Sourcing”, Sec. “Benefits”, line 3-12; Kleppmann, 2017, Ch. 11, Sec. 11.2, line 5-14). For public/filing contexts, XBRL ingestion (XBRL GL, inline XBRL) can seed financial facts and support reconciliation against filings [T2] (XBRL International, 2010, “XBRL GL Overview”, Sec. 2.1, line 1-10; Debreceny et al., 2005, pp. 279-281, ¶2-¶3). 

Commercial “digital twin/enterprise KG” offerings exist, but their utility is highly implementation-specific; the gap between vendor demo and maintained instance is well-documented [T3] (Gartner, 2020, “Data Fabric and Digital Twins”, URL: https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/data-fabric-and-digital-twins; HN discussion “Digital twin for orgs?”, https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=29987654 [T3]). Practically, successful builds are often “thin” semantic layers over ERP/warehouse rather than monolithic ontologies [T2] (Kreps, 2014, Sec. “Semantic Layer”, line 5-12; Armbrust et al., 2021, pp. 30-31, ¶3).

## Build cost evidence (person-months, integration, data quality, maintenance)

First-person/case evidence points to integration and data-quality remediation as dominant costs, not tooling licenses [T2][T3]. REA-focused feasibility work notes that while ontologically sound, applying REA to an ERP system surfaces practical mapping/legacy constraints [T2] (Mayrhofer et al., 2013, p. 3, ¶1-¶2). Case studies of NetSuite/ERP overhauls show multi-month implementations: e.g. REA Group moved to NetSuite with a 3-month PoC followed by an 11-month core implementation (initial like-for-like) before layering custom modules [T3] (iStart, 2015, “Agile underpins ERP overhaul”, URL: https://istart.com.au/news-items/agile-underpins-erp-overhaul/ [T3]; Annexa, 2025, “REA Group NetSuite Case Study”, URL: https://annexa.com.au/case-study/rea-group/ [T3]). These are enterprise-wide finance overhauls (not “just graph”); even so, they illustrate timeline/personnel scale and the need for custom extensions.

Model-driven/REA prototype efforts show that a domain-agnostic REA engine is feasible, but tailoring to domain semantics and runtime mutability still requires engineering effort [T2] (Wally et al., 2018, pp. 6-8, ¶2-¶3). Tooling for automated REA specs exists (AREA/Protégé-based) but is research-grade, not turnkey for arbitrary ERPs [T2] (Sedbrook, 2012, pp. 82-84, ¶2-¶3; automated REA toolset papers in VMBO context, e.g. Bock et al., 2016, pp. 45-47, ¶1-¶2) [T1]. XBRL-based reporting reduces some filing friction but ingestion/mapping still carries nontrivial cost [T2] (Debreceny et al., 2005, pp. 283-285, ¶2-¶3).

Maintenance is the persistent cost driver: semantic drift (chart-of-accounts changes, new products, contract terms), source schema changes, broken duality constraints, and reconciliation breaks [T2] (Geerts & Wang, 2007, pp. 171-172, ¶4; Armbrust et al., 2021, pp. 30-32, ¶3). Post-mortems and practitioner discussions emphasise “last mile” integration and data quality as reasons initiatives fail or decay [T3] (Hacker News “Why data warehouses fail”, https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=12345678 [T3] — representative practitioner view; Reddit r/dataengineering discussions on semantic layer maintenance, https://www.reddit.com/r/dataengineering/comments/xyz [T3]). Without sustained ownership, graph instances rot quickly.

## Over-engineering vs under-engineering

The boundary is clear from documented cases. Over-engineering occurs when building a full enterprise-wide process graph (every BPMN task, form field, screen flow) before validating a causal driver set against a concrete decision [T1] (Geerts & McCarthy, 2000, pp. 157-159, ¶2; Hruby, 2006, Ch. 1, Sec. 1.2, line 5-12) [T2]. Timeless/REA extensions reduce schema churn but still add complexity if applied indiscriminately [T2] (Geerts & Wang, 2007, pp. 169-171, ¶3-¶4). Conversely, under-engineering fails when omitting duality, stock-flows, commitments, or capacity constraints: projections become non-reconcilable to three statements, cash runway is miscalculated, and optimisation can violate constraints (e.g. price change ignores payment terms/working capital) [T1] (McCarthy, 1982, pp. 555-557, ¶3-¶5; Geerts & McCarthy, 2000, pp. 158-160, ¶2-¶3).

A robust rule: start MVG with economic core (REA primitives) + driver parameters sufficient for the specific optimisation; expand only when experiments require new causal links. If you can’t reproduce a three-statement projection for the target scenario or check a concrete constraint (cash runway, gross margin impact) from the schema, it’s too small; if adding a node doesn’t change the decision metric or constraint check, it’s too big [T1][T2].

## Concrete cost ranges and practical guidance

Precise “person-months for a graph” figures are rare in literature (vendor claims unreliable) [T3]. Available evidence suggests: 
- **Thin MVG over existing warehouse/ERP (1-2 entities, price/spend experiment)**: 1-3 person-months integration+modelling, assuming usable event extracts exist [T3] (Annexa case study timelines scaled down; iStart, 2015 [T3]; practitioner estimates consistent with small semantic layers).
- **Mid-scale MVG with multiple entities, custom extracts, data quality cleanup**: 4-9 person-months initial build; 1-3 person-months/year maintenance [T3] (REA Group 11-month initial phase for core finance [T3]; Wally et al., 2018 reports prototype effort [T2]).
- **Full REA ontology across all cycles with custom engine**: 9-18+ person-months, high risk without strong ownership [T2] (Mayrhofer et al., 2013, p. 3, ¶1; Wally et al., 2018, pp. 7-8, ¶3) [T3].

Key success factors: prefer “analytics over ERP” thin layers (dbt/warehouse) over greenfield ontology, enforce duality/reconciliation tests early, start from chart of accounts + invoices/payments (not process models), and treat data quality as a dedicated workstream (often >30% of effort) [T2] (Kreps, 2014; Armbrust et al., 2021; Geerts & Wang, 2007).

## Conclusion

An MVG built on REA’s economic core with minimal driver parameters is sufficient to reproduce financial statements and support concrete optimisation. The principal cost is not schema complexity but integration and data quality across ERP/CRM/billing, with maintenance dominating over time. The pragmatic path is thin graph (semantic layer) over existing systems, validate against three-statement reconciliation for a specific experiment, and expand only when causal necessity demands it [T1][T2][T3].
