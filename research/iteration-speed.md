# Iteration Speed as a First-Class Variable in the Business-as-Machine Model

## Two distinct clocks: rate of change and feedback delay

A business treated as a transformation machine needs two timing variables that are routinely conflated. The first is **rate of change**: how fast the firm can reconfigure its product or operations, whose reciprocal is cycle time / lead time. The second is **feedback delay**: how long the market's reaction to a released change takes to reach a decision node. A machine can deploy quickly and still learn slowly if its feedback plumbing is poor. The distinction matters because each is a different edge on the graph: rate of change is a property of transformation nodes and the queues in front of them; feedback delay is a property of measurement edges running from the market back to decision points.

The DevOps Research and Assessment (DORA) program built its measurement layer around exactly these two clocks. DORA's throughput metrics (change lead time — "the amount of time it takes for a change to go from committed to version control to deployed in production" — and deployment frequency) measure rate of change, while its instability metrics (change fail rate, deployment rework rate) price the consequences of that rate [T2] DORA 2023 report, "Concepts and measures", ¶1; DORA "DORA's software delivery performance metrics" guide, "Throughput", ¶1. Tutorials and reports repeatedly return to "fast feedback" as the reason to pursue fast flow: the 2023 report frames "we need to work to establish fast feedback and recover from failures very quickly" [T2] DORA 2023 report, "Operational performance" ¶3.

Two further properties deserve explicit representation. First, **flow**: by Little's law the average work-in-process (WIP) equals throughput times cycle time; WIP is therefore a stock — a pile of half-transformed assets sitting between transformation nodes — not an inert queue but real capital tied up in the graph [T2] Reinertsen 2009, ch. 6 "Applying WIP Constraints", ¶1. Second, **tempo**: Sequoia's Mike Vernal decomposes product-market-fit pace into speed *and* consistency, arguing founders should set an explicit cadence for "shipping and getting feedback" at every stage rather than merely sprinting [T3] TechCrunch, "Sequoia's Mike Vernal outlines how to design feedback loops", https://techcrunch.com/2021/07/23/sequoias-mike-vernal-outlines-how-to-design-feedback-loops-in-the-search-for-product-market-fit, §Tempo ¶2–4. In queueing terms, cadence stabilises the arrival pattern so that cycle-time variance, not just its mean, is managed — and in physical supply chains it is precisely lead-time *variance* that hurts financial performance more than the mean [T1] Ponte et al. 2018, p. 166, ¶2.

The economic weight of these clocks is rarely quantified, which is itself diagnostic. Reinertsen's surveys found that only about 15% of product developers can state the cost of delay of their projects, only 2% measure queue lengths, and only 3% are actively reducing batch size — which is why queues grow silently [T2] Reinertsen 2009, ch. 1, ¶11–14. His prescription — "If you only quantify one thing, quantify the cost of delay" — converts delay into a priced property of graph edges, so prioritisation reduces to an economic comparison rather than an intuition [T2] PMI, "What is the economic cost of delay for software delivery", ¶1–3.

## Evidence that speed and feedback latency predict survival, growth, and value

The empirical case for treating iteration speed as a first-class variable, not a taste, rests on several independent bodies of data.

**Software delivery performance predicts organisational performance.** Across nine years and 36,000+ surveyed professionals, DORA finds that "an organization's level of software delivery performance predicts overall performance, team performance, and employee well-being" [T2] DORA 2023 report page, "Key findings" ¶1; [T2] Google Cloud blog, "Announcing the 2023 State of DevOps Report", ¶3. The throughput/stability multipliers between elite and low performers are extreme by any cross-industry standard: 127× faster change lead time, 8× lower change failure rate, 182× more deployments per year, and 2,293× faster recovery from failed deployments [T2] DORA 2024 report, "Software delivery performance", p. 15, ¶1. The 2023 benchmark bands give the model concrete numbers to compare against: elite = on-demand deployments, sub-day change lead time, <5% change failure rate, sub-hour failed-deployment recovery [T2] DORA 2023 infographic, "Software delivery performance", ¶1.

**Development velocity correlates with revenue.** McKinsey's Developer Velocity Index (DVI), an aggregate of technology, working practices, and organisational enablement, found top-quartile firms grew revenue **4–5× faster** than bottom-quartile peers over 2014–2018 (n=239), with the strongest DVI–performance correlations for innovation (r≈0.6) and customer satisfaction (r≈0.6) [T2] McKinsey 2020, "Developer Velocity: How software excellence fuels business performance", Exhibit 1–2; [T2] McKinsey 2021, "How industry leaders excel at software development", ¶2. This is correlational, but it shows the ability-to-change fast is priced like a growth driver.

**Being late has a measurable market-value price.** In an event study of 101 firms, announcements of delayed new-product introductions reduced market value by 5.25% on average (≈$119m in 1991 dollars), and the penalty was steeper in more competitive industries [T1] Hendricks & Singhal 1997, p. 422, ¶1. A companion study shows the same delays degrade operating performance over time [T1] Hendricks & Singhal 2008, p. 878, ¶1.

**Meta-analyses of NPD speed.** Across the innovation literature, development speed is generally positively associated with new-product success, but the association is contingent: it "may diminish or even disappear depending upon methodological design decisions and research contexts" [T1] Cankurtaran et al. 2013, p. 465, ¶1. Speed's antecedents — structure, team, process — are themselves well-mapped [T1] Chen, Damanpour & Reilly 2010, p. 17, ¶1; and the literature explicitly flags that extreme speed can be "too much of a good thing", which the graph model must respect (see §5) [T1] Chen, Reilly & Lynn 2012, p. 288, ¶1.

**Ambidexterity as the balanced variant.** Organisations that run both exploration (fast, uncertain change) and exploitation (stable, efficient operation) show a positive overall performance relationship in meta-analysis, though stronger in non-manufacturing industries and at higher levels of analysis [T1] Junni et al. 2013, p. 299, ¶1. The tension March identified — that adaptive systems refine exploitation faster than exploration and so become "effective in the short run but self-destructive in the long run" — is a warning about letting iteration speed optimise only the local loop [T1] March 1991, p. 71, ¶3.

**Startup survival data.** Startup Genome's analysis of 3,200+ high-growth startups found that startups pivoting once or twice raise 2.5× more money, grow users 3.6× faster, and are 52% less likely to scale prematurely than those pivoting ≥3 times or never [T2] Startup Genome Report v2 (2011/12), "Why startups succeed", key findings ¶1, ¶3. Metric-tracking companies grow ~7× faster monthly and are 60% more likely to raise funding [T2] Startup Genome Report v2, "Listen to customer feedback", ¶1. Conversely, 70% of failed startups showed premature scaling; none that scaled prematurely passed 100,000 users, 93% never crossed $100k monthly revenue, and properly-scaling startups grew ~20× faster [T2] Startup Genome, "Premature Scaling: A Deep Dive", ¶1–3. The lesson cuts both ways: too slow to learn, or too fast to commit-at-scale, both kill — the variable must be tuned, not maximised.

**Controlled evidence for the lean-startup loop.** Two empirical/analytic pillars support the build-measure-learn mechanism. In NSF I-Corps data across 152 teams, the lean startup's components — hypothesis formulation, customer probing, idea convergence — operate as advertised, and greater engagement with the method predicted higher firm performance 18 months post-intervention [T1] Leatherbee & Katila 2020, p. 583, ¶1. Formally, the optimal MVP is neither too high nor too low in quality, and is aimed either at confirming the likely product design or ruling out the less likely one — i.e., the *information value* of a test, not its output, is what the loop maximises [T1] Yoo, Huang & Arifoğlu 2021, p. 395, ¶1–2. But the same literature shows the loop's blindness: a heavy emphasis on "readily observable feedback and immediately validated learning" pushes teams toward validating only where it is easy to observe, generating incremental rather than novel value [T1] Felin et al. 2020, p. 101889, ¶2. Iteration velocity without a theory to test just optimises the wrong thing faster.

## Flow mechanics: the levers that set the rate of change

The measurable levers of rate of change, plus the benchmark data to compare them, are well established. DORA's five metrics (change lead time, deployment frequency, failed deployment recovery time, change fail rate, deployment rework rate) split into "throughput" and "instability", and act as leading indicators for organisational performance and lagging indicators for engineering practices [T2] DORA, "DORA's software delivery performance metrics" guide, ¶1, "Throughput"–"Instability". Successful policy for changing all of them at once is **small batches**: "a common approach to improving all four measures is reducing the batch size of changes… smaller changes are easier to reason about and to move through the delivery process" [T2] DORA 2023 report, "Key outcomes", ¶4. Tooling computes these mechanically — e.g., Apache DevLake derives median change lead time from the PR-to-deployment interval and bins teams against DORA's elite/high/medium/low bands [T2] Apache DevLake docs, "Median Lead Time for Changes", ¶1–2.

Underneath the telemetry sit queueing laws. Reducing WIP (the stock of in-flight transformations) is the direct lever on cycle time; its converse — high utilisation — creates queues whose cost is invisible unless the cost-of-delay is priced [T2] Reinertsen 2009, ch. 1, ¶10–14. Batch size sits on a U-curve: smaller batches cut cycle time, risk and feedback latency, but transaction costs rise as batches shrink past the optimum; therefore "cutting batch sizes leads to speed and lower cost at the same time" only below the corner of the curve [T2] PDMA Book Review of *The Principles of Product Development Flow*, ¶3; [T2] Reinertsen 2009, ch. 5 "The Case for Batch Size Reduction", ¶1. Cadence (a regular release rhythm) and decentralised control round out the toolkit, converting rate of change from a property of individuals into a designed system parameter [T2] Reinertsen 2009, ch. 6, ¶1.

For generalising to physical goods and services, supply-chain research shows lead-time reduction and stabilisation improve both internal operations and customer-facing performance — shorter, steadier lead times raise throughput and cut the safety stock that must buffer uncertainty [T1] Ponte et al. 2018, p. 165, ¶1. DORA's own guidance is explicit that its metrics "work for any type of technology," including mainframe travel systems and retail banking — i.e., the measurement schema transfers across delivery contexts, though it is scoped per application, not blended across the firm [T2] DORA, "DORA's software delivery performance metrics" guide, "Key insights", ¶3.

Two measurement pitfalls belong in the model. (1) Because the metrics are both leading and lagging, teams that "set metrics as a goal" trigger Goodhart's law; DORA cautions against mandate statements like "every application must deploy multiple times per day" [T2] DORA, "DORA's software delivery performance metrics" guide, "Common pitfalls", ¶1. (2) Comparing metrics "between vastly different applications can be misleading" [T2] DORA, "DORA's software delivery performance metrics" guide, "Common pitfalls", ¶5. Both are warnings for the optimiser: measure at the node level, benchmark against structurally comparable peers, and use the metric to guide, not to command.

## The learning loop: PDCA, OODA, and experimentation velocity

The rate at which a business changes is only useful if paired with a loop that converts released changes into tested beliefs. Three canonical loop models converge on the same physics.

**PDCA/PDSA.** The plan–do–check–act cycle descends from Shewhart's 1939 specification–production–inspection cycle; Japanese executives recast Deming's 1950 "Deming Wheel" into PDCA, and Deming later insisted on *study* rather than *check* — "check" means "to hold back", while *study* is a theory-driven comparison of prediction against result [T1] Moen & Norman 2010, pp. 3–8, ¶1–4; [T2] The Deming Institute, "PDSA Cycle", ¶2–3. Its defining property is iteration: "each cycle closer than the previous," converging on better system knowledge [T1] Moen & Norman 2010, p. 8, ¶2. That is the learning-rate kernel of the machine: one PDSA lap equals one unit of validated learning.

**OODA.** Boyd's observe–orient–decide–act loop adds a competitive dimension: victory comes from "getting inside the enemy's decision cycle" — executing your own loop faster than the adversary can process your moves, generating confusion and disorder in theirs [T2] PMI, "Timely Decisions", ¶1–4. Orientation, not speed per se, is the schwerpunkt of Boyd's loop: "orientation shapes the way we observe, the way we decide, the way we act" [T3] oodaloop.com, "The OODA Loop Explained", §The importance of orient, https://www.oodaloop.com/the-ooda-loop-explained-the-real-story-about-the-ultimate-model-for-decision-making-in-competitive-environments, ¶8. The modern warning from military doctrine is directly relevant to an optimiser: compressing observe+orient into automated *recognition*, and decide into *prediction*, collapses the loop into a predict–act vector that "trades the reflection and adaptability of the circle for the velocity of the arrow" — faster, but capable of bypassing orientation entirely [T2] U.S. Army War College, "Untying the OODA Loop", ¶5–7.

**Build–measure–learn / experimentation velocity.** The lean-startup method operationalises all of the above as product-market-fit machinery: a minimum viable product is released, its feedback absorbed, and the design pivoted accordingly [T1] Yoo, Huang & Arifoğlu 2021, p. 395, ¶1. Its effectiveness is boundary-conditioned: the formal model identifies the product-market conditions under which testing beats planning [T1] Yoo, Huang & Arifoğlu 2021, p. 395, ¶2, and the critique literature shows that a feedback loop is only as good as the theory it tests [T1] Felin et al. 2020, p. 101889, ¶2.

At scale, experimentation velocity is a deliberate capability. Booking.com runs roughly 25,000 controlled tests per year with ~75% of core employees empowered to test autonomously, transforming the firm into the largest online hotel platform; Amazon, Microsoft, and Netflix run tens of thousands of experiments annually [T2] Thomke, *Experimentation Works* (2020), ch. "The experimentation organization", ¶1; [T2] HBR, "Building a Culture of Experimentation" (Mar–Apr 2020), Summary, ¶1. The yield statistics are sobering and belong in the model: on average only about one in ten experiments improves the bottom line, so the *number* of cycles is what produces wins — "multiplied by 1,000 experiments a year means 100 wins" [T2] HBS Working Knowledge, "Creating the Experimentation Organization", ¶5. This is a pure law-of-large-numbers argument for short cycles: with a ~10% hit rate, experiment count is a first-order growth parameter, not a boutique preference.

## Conway's law and team topologies: the organisation graph as the binding constraint

Rate of change is bounded from above by the structure that produces the change. Conway's law states that "organizations which design systems … are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations" [T1] Conway 1968, p. 30, ¶4; and the original paper's corollary — "flexibility of organization is important to effective design" — is the lever on the constraint [T1] Conway 1968, p. 31, ¶1. The modern restatement is blunter: the design organisation structure "doesn't just direct the design, but actually constrains the set of designs that can be contemplated" [T2] ACM Queue, "How Do Committees Invent? and Ironies of Automation", ¶4. On the transformation graph this is a hard constraint: the team graph and the system graph are mirror images, so changing one without the other is changing the machine's shape without its skeleton.

Team Topologies systematises the design of the communication graph for fast flow, around three principles — "fast flow, rapid feedback and limits on team cognitive load" — four team types (stream-aligned, platform, enabling, complicated-subsystem) and three interaction modes [T2] Skelton & Pais, *Team Topologies* (2019), blurb, ¶1; [T3] Thoughtworks Technology Podcast, "Team topologies and effective software delivery", transcript ¶10. The load constraint is cognitive, not just structural: software that exceeds a team's cognitive capacity "works against organisational agility", so service size should be bounded by the team's intrinsic/extraneous/germane cognitive load budget, with platform teams existing chiefly to reduce load on stream-aligned teams [T3] InfoQ, "DOES London: Team Topologies and Cognitive Load", ¶2–4. DORA independently validates the loose-coupling half: "continuous integration, loosely coupled teams, and fast code reviews significantly improve software delivery and operational performance" [T2] DORA 2023 report page, "Key findings" ¶1. The practical corollary — the so-called *inverse Conway maneuver*, deliberately restructuring teams to produce the desired system structure — is the standard way organisations raise their ceiling on rate of change [T2] Forsgren, Humble & Kim, *Accelerate* (2018), ch. 11 "The Capabilities of Teams", ¶1.

The model implication: the organisation graph is not scenery. Iteration-speed parameters are owned by teams with bounded cognitive load, and every cross-team interface adds a communication and handoff edge that lengthens lead time. Optimising rate of change without co-optimising the team graph fails Conway's constraint.

## Trade-offs: agility versus stability, cost, and constraint violations

The evidence cuts against "speed is always good." Three trade-off families must be priced into the machine.

**Speed vs quality/profit.** The NPD meta-analytic evidence shows speed's benefits diminish in specific contexts [T1] Cankurtaran et al. 2013, p. 465, ¶1, and the "too much of a good thing" framing flags curvilinearity [T1] Chen, Reilly & Lynn 2012, p. 288, ¶1. Industry-specific work finds speed relating *less* positively to profit when products are highly novel to the firm ("new-to-the-firm" innovation demands "greater experimentation and more numerous iterations"), and more positively to quality for innovative products — i.e., the speed-value link is strongest where the firm iterates, not where it rushes novel development [T1] "Speed to Market for Innovative Products: Blessing or Curse?", J. Product Innovation Mgmt 2012, p. 751–752, ¶2. Within the delivery firm, DORA observes that "speed and stability are not tradeoffs" *for top performers*, who score high on both throughput and instability metrics simultaneously — but this is achieved through small batches and automated safety nets, not through sacrificing either axis [T2] DORA, "DORA's software delivery performance metrics" guide, "Key insights" ¶1.

**Batch-size economics.** Small batches are fast and low-risk but incur transaction costs; the optimal batch minimises the sum of holding cost and transaction cost [T2] PDMA Book Review, ¶3. Pushing cycle time to zero on every node is therefore not optimal — it trades the machine's shared resource attention away from larger-batch production and against queue stability.

**Constraint violations and premature scaling.** Agility can outrun the constraints the machine is meant to respect. The Startup Genome data is the clearest evidence: scaling velocity *before* problem-solution fit is validated — spending on team, product, marketing ahead of evidence — is the dominant self-inflicted failure mode (70% of failed startups), and it is a *speed of commitment* error, not a speed-of-learning one [T2] Startup Genome, "Premature Scaling: A Deep Dive", ¶1–2. Similarly, experimentation can be distorted by external constraints: when founders need funding between tests, investors infer quality from test outcomes, which "can incentivize the entrepreneur to distort his experimentation strategy," prioritising funding over learning or discarding promising innovations to avoid false positives [T1] Sudhir, Yoo & Zhou 2025, Marketing Science, arXiv-style abstract ¶1–2. And March's formal result is the deepest version of the trade-off: systems that optimise exploitation narrowly "suffer the costs of experimentation without gaining many of its benefits" on the exploration side, and vice versa — the two loops compete for scarce resources [T1] March 1991, p. 71, ¶2–3. An optimiser that maximises loop speed on the current local graph without devoting a share of cycles to exploration converges to a local optimum and strands the business when the environment moves.

## Representing rate of change and feedback delay on the transformation graph

This section gives concrete schema: where the two timing variables attach, and how they thread into the P&L/balance-sheet layer.

**Node parameters.** Every transformation node *T* (a product team, a manufacturing cell, a marketing function) is measured by four quantities: cycle time τ(T) (time one unit of change spends inside the node); batch size β(T); capacity/utilization u(T); and yield γ(T) (successful conversions per attempted change). Every "deploy/launch" edge *T→market* carries deployment frequency f. The firm-wide rate of change is a **Change-Capacity capability node** whose value is bottlenecked by the slowest constrained team — equivalent to "raising the constraint raises throughput" in factory terms, and to DORA's advice to "identify the most significant constraint or bottleneck" and commit the whole team to fixing it [T2] DORA, "DORA's software delivery performance metrics" guide, "Next steps", ¶2.

**Edge parameters.** Feedback edges (market metrics, NPS, revenue, churn, experiments) carry a delay δ(F): the latency from a released change to an observed, causally attributable metric response. This is distinguished from lead time because a change can be deployed in minutes yet take months to be *learned from* (long analysis latency, weak instrumentation). Learning rate per clock period = 1/(τ + δ + decision latency). The number of learning cycles schedulable within remaining runway is:

```
cycles_left = max(0, runway / (experiment_cost + iteration_cost))
learned_per_period = 1 / (τ(T) + δ(F) + d)
```

This is the single most important scalar in the model for survival: if `learned_per_period` is too low, the machine cannot validate before cash runs out [T3] TechCrunch, Vernal, https://techcrunch.com/2021/07/23/sequoias-mike-vernal-outlines-how-to-design-feedback-loops-in-the-search-for-product-market-fit, §Tempo ¶4.

**Stocks.** WIP, holding to Little's law WIP = rate × τ, is a real balance-sheet stock: half-transformed assets are inventory, and the cash conversion cycle stretches as WIP grows [T2] Reinertsen 2009, ch. 6, ¶1. Knowledge/learning is itself an asset node that accumulates along feedback edges; its drawdown rate is the learning rate above, and its depreciation is the "distance in time and space between the locus of learning and the locus for the realization of returns" noted for exploration [T1] March 1991, p. 72, ¶1. Experiment and experimentation-platform costs are operating expenses attached to the feedback-edge subgraph; at a ~10% experiment yield, expected value added per cycle is 0.1× (expected unit economics of a winning change), so opex on feedback infrastructure is an investment in the count of cycles [T2] HBS Working Knowledge, "Creating the Experimentation Organization", ¶5.

**Trade-off surfaces.** The model should store each node's agility-stability frontier as a policy curve — batch size β vs change failure rate and recovery time (empirically, elites are simultaneously fastest and safest [T2] DORA, "DORA's software delivery performance metrics" guide, "Key insights" ¶1) — and each constraint as a predicate: compliance approval latency, review latency, safety/regulation gates, brand-risk holdbacks all add **lead-time edges** to the graph and reduce effective cycle time from τ to τ + Σ_constraint_delays. Cost of delay, priced per edge, turns scheduling into an economic computation and is the missing weight in most optimisation formulations [T2] PMI, "What is the economic cost of delay", ¶2–3.

**Benchmarks.** Each layer of the graph has its comparative yardsticks: DORA performer bands (elite/high/medium/low) for delivery nodes [T2] Apache DevLake, "Deployment Frequency", ¶1; DVI quartiles for whole-firm development capability [T2] McKinsey 2020, Exhibit 1; Startup Genome stage-appropriateness flags (discovery/validation/scaling) guarding against premature-scaling constraint violations [T2] Startup Genome, "Premature Scaling: A Deep Dive", ¶2; and supply-chain lead-time reduction norms for physical transformation nodes [T1] Ponte et al. 2018, p. 165, ¶1. The harness can therefore benchmark "how fast can this node change" against literally comparable peers, interpolate an optimal rate of change, and flag when a proposed initiative would violate a constraint faster than validation can keep up.
