# Modelling Constraints: Cash, Legal, Mission, Values, and Brand

An optimiser that searches a business's transformation graph for higher throughput, lower cost, or faster feedback will happily propose plans that are illegal, insolvent, or brand-destroying unless constraints are first-class objects in the model. The task is therefore not to bolt a checklist onto the optimiser but to represent every limit the business operates under — liquidity floors, debt covenants, tax and regulation, capacity, mission, values, brand promises, ESG commitments — as typed, machine-checkable predicates that are attached to specific nodes and edges and that the search is forced to respect. This file sets out how those constraints divide into hard and soft classes, how they attach to a resource–event–agent graph, how each major category is formalised, how they are enforced at runtime against an autonomous agent, and how violations and trade-offs are surfaced to a human or agentic strategy advisor.

## 1. Two constraint classes: feasibility bounds and preference functions

The foundational distinction is between constraints that *must* hold and constraints that *should* hold. In constraint-satisfaction terms, "hard constraints must be satisfied; soft constraints should be satisfied, but may be violated" — a teacher cannot teach two classes at once, but "the schedule should be concentrated in two days" is merely preferred [T1] Wölfl, Nebel & Becker-Asano 2015, Constraint Optimization, §"Hard and soft constraints", ¶1. Formalising both yields a constraint-optimization problem: a constraint network of hard constraints augmented with a global cost function that aggregates the satisfaction of soft constraints [T1] Wölfl et al. 2015, §"Constraint optimization", ¶2. A hard constraint is best understood as the limiting case of a soft constraint whose violation cost is infinite (a "forbidden" tuple) [T3] Wikipedia, "Weighted constraint satisfaction problem", ¶2, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighted_constraint_satisfaction_problem.

Four generic models organise how soft constraints are represented and combined: constraint hierarchies (ranked levels), partial constraint satisfaction (problem weakening), valued CSP (a valuation attached to each constraint), and semiring-based CSP (a preference set with two combinators) [T1] Barták 2002, "Modelling Soft Constraints: A Survey", §Introduction, ¶2–3. The choice of combining operator is not cosmetic: additive combination (weighted CSP) sums violation costs, max/`min` combination (fuzzy CSP) treats the worst violation as dominant, and the semiring formulation induces only a *partial* order, which is what makes it usable for genuinely multi-criteria decisions [T1] Barták 2002, §6, ¶2; [T1] Schiex, "Soft constraint processing" (ECAI 2004 tutorial), §"Specific frameworks". In practical systems the "pragmatic approach" is to model preference as an optimisation problem with penalties [T1] Barták 2002, §8, ¶1 — which is precisely how a business should treat soft constraints: EBITDA or mission-drift become cost terms, not vetoes.

Mapping to the business machine: the cash floor and tax liabilities are hard (infinite-cost) constraints; a debt covenant is hard at its test date but its *headroom* is a soft early-warning signal; a mission or brand promise is a soft constraint with a high weight that should dominate the objective in the relevant region of the solution space. Choosing additive versus min-max aggregation encodes organisational risk appetite: a min-max (fuzzy) formulation says "never let any single soft constraint be catastrophically violated", while additive says "a large gain elsewhere may justify a moderate violation here."

## 2. Attaching constraints to nodes and edges of the graph

Constraints are only executable if the graph has a stable ontology to attach them to. The Resource–Event–Agent (REA) model is the natural candidate: it represents economic resources (scarce things under the control of an agent), economic events (phenomena that affect the value of resources, such as production, exchange, and consumption), and economic agents (persons and organisations that participate in or are responsible for events) [T1] Geerts & McCarthy 2002, "An ontological analysis of the economic primitives of the extended-REA enterprise information architecture", §Introduction; [T3] Wikipedia, "Resources, events, agents (accounting model)", ¶3, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resources,_events,_agents_(accounting_model). Its relationships are typed: stock-flow (use, consume, give, take, produce), duality (a decrement event paired with an increment event), and participation (inside/outside agents) [T2] Geerts & McCarthy 2000, "The Ontological Foundation of REA Enterprise Information Systems", §"Operational Infrastructure Exchange", ¶4. Crucially, REA extends upward to a value-chain and value-system level and adds *commitments* — "an agreement to execute an economic event in a well-defined future that will result in either an increase or a decrease of resources" — linked to actual events by a fulfilment relation [T2] Geerts & McCarthy 2000, §"Commitment", ¶1; [T1] Dunn, Gerard & Grabski, "Resources-Events-Agents Design Theory", CAIS, §"REA history", ¶2. Commitments are exactly where planned constraints (a signed supply contract, a financing draw, an emission-reduction pledge) live on the graph.

Because REA is a domain ontology rather than a diagram, its rules can be made *executable*. The REA2 axiomatisation formalises the stock-flow and duality axioms as finite-state machines, so that "all economic resources in ownership should have an inflow and must eventually have an outflow" and "all events effecting an outflow must eventually be paired in duality relationships with events effecting an inflow" become checkable invariants [T1] Laurier, "An executable axiomatization of the REA2 ontology", §"REA axioms as FSMs", ¶1–2. This is the template for node- and edge-level constraints: a resource node carries capacity and stock bounds; an event node carries transaction legality and value limits; an agent node carries authority and participation rules; an edge carries flow-consistency (duality/stock-flow) rules.

The Decision Model and Notation (DMN) standard shows how to attach decision logic to such a graph in a business-readable way. A Decision Requirements Graph models a domain of decision-making as nodes for decisions, business knowledge models (reusable functions), input data, and knowledge sources — where a knowledge source "denotes an authority for a Business Knowledge Model or Decision", i.e. the natural attachment point for a regulation or policy [T2] OMG, "Decision Model and Notation (DMN), v1.5", §5, ¶"Knowledge Source" and §6.2. DMN decision tables are deterministic; the cDMN extension adds *constraint tables* whose rows are logical implications and whose combined semantics define a solution space rather than a single output, and which map to first-order logic feedable to solvers such as z3 and MiniSat [T1] Calvanese et al. 2020, "Tackling the DMN Challenges with cDMN", arXiv:2005.09998, §3, ¶2 and §5, ¶1. A constraint table row such as "if the design is closed, material M2 cannot be used" is precisely the shape a business rule should take — an implication attached to a scope, not a prescription of a single value.

A practical schema can therefore attach every constraint to the graph with a small, fixed set of fields:

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `scope` | Graph element: resource/event/agent/commitment node id, or edge id (stock-flow, duality, participation) |
| `class` | `hard` (infinite cost) or `soft` (valued) |
| `category` | liquidity, covenant, tax, legal, capacity, mission, values, brand, ESG |
| `predicate` | Boolean or valued expression over model state |
| `parameters` | Thresholds, weights, deadlines, jurisdiction/scope conditions |
| `timing` | Always, per test date, or triggered by an event |
| `enforcement` | `observe` / `warn` / `escalate` / `modify` / `deny` |
| `evidence` | Required audit artefact (for legal/regulatory constraints) |

The enforcement vocabulary is drawn from agent runtime-enforcement systems (§6), which makes the same object usable both for offline optimisation and for live agent control.

## 3. Hard financial constraints: liquidity, runway, covenants, tax, capacity

Cash is the constraint that most often binds first. Runway should be computed from a monthly integrated cash model and split into three measures: *gross runway* (until cash reaches zero), *usable runway* (until unrestricted cash hits a board-approved minimum or other decision threshold), and *covenant runway* (until the earliest forecast covenant breach); the shortest of these governs the financing-action calendar [T2] Matchpoint Partners, "The Venture-Debt Runway for UK AI Companies", §7, ¶1–2 and §"covenants", ¶"Headroom should be measured in time and value". The minimum-liquidity threshold should reflect payroll, taxes, critical suppliers, service continuity, covenant headroom, notice periods, and the time required to raise alternative capital — i.e. it is a node-level hard predicate, `cash(t) ≥ M_min`, whose parameter is itself a function of the business's obligations [T2] Matchpoint Partners, §7, ¶2.

Debt covenants are the second family and come in three types: leverage covenants (a maximum net-debt/EBITDA ratio), coverage covenants (a minimum interest or fixed-charge coverage ratio), and minimum-liquidity covenants (a minimum cash or undrawn-facility balance) [T3] Oxxon Advisors, "Covenant Analysis and Headroom", ¶2, https://www.oxxonadvisors.com/fmae-knowledge-centre/technical-guides/covenant-analysis-and-headroom/. Each is a hard inequality at each test date — `leverage(t) ≤ L_max`, `coverage(t) ≥ C_min` — but the more informative object is *headroom*, the buffer between the projected metric and the threshold, calculated at every scheduled test date and under a downside scenario, with the period of minimum headroom flagged as the key risk [T3] Oxxon Advisors, ¶"Covenant headroom" and ¶"Headroom under stress"; [T3] Advanced Modeling Institute, "How Financial Covenants Test Credit Risk", ¶"This forward-looking view…", https://www.advancedmodelinginstitute.com/learn/how-financial-covenants-test-credit-risk/. Sign conventions matter: leverage is a maximum test, coverage and cash are minimum tests, and a model that uses the wrong sign will flag a healthy result as a breach [T3] Advanced Modeling Institute, §"The comparison operator must reflect the covenant definition", ¶1. A treasury tool demonstrates the executable form: cash runway, leverage, coverage, additional debt capacity before breach, interest-expense buffer, and total liquidity runway are each returned as deterministic functions of current cash, burn, minimum reserve, debt, EBITDA, interest, covenant ceilings, and available credit [T3] Quarantadue AI, "Treasury MCP Tool", §FR-001–FR-006, https://quarantadue.ai/treasury/software/11.5.1. These six functions are ready-made node constraints for a financing sub-graph.

Covenants should be modelled as embedded real options: a firm with a Debt/EBITDA covenant is effectively short a put option on its own assets, facing penalties on breach, with a "downgrade asset level" at which the covenant is hit [T1] "The Dynamic Covenant Capital Structure (DCCS) Model", SSRN 5029708, abstract. The optimal restrictiveness of a covenant is not arbitrary: it is less likely to be included when growth opportunities, earnings volatility, drift, interest rate, and tax rate are higher, and more likely when leverage (bankruptcy risk) is greater [T1] Sarkar 2023, "Optimal restrictiveness of a financing covenant", Journal of Banking & Finance 150, abstract. Empirically, volatile *transitory* cash-flow shocks are associated with *fewer* liquidity covenants (flexibility to survive crunches), whereas volatile *permanent* cash flow is associated with more, because it signals weaker fundamentals [T1] Ma et al. 2025, "Transitory and Permanent Cash Flow Shocks in Debt Contract Design", Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, abstract. The lesson for the model is that covenant parameters should be estimated from the business's cash-flow persistence, not copied as constants.

Capacity is the physical analogue. The theory of constraints holds that any manageable system is limited by a very small number of constraints, and offers the five focusing steps: identify the constraint, exploit it, subordinate everything else to it, elevate it, and repeat [T3] Wikipedia, "Theory of constraints", §"The five focusing steps", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_constraints; [T2] SAP, "Theory of Constraints" documentation, §"drum-buffer-rope", ¶1. Throughput accounting measures the system by throughput (sales minus totally variable cost), inventory (money invested in things intended for sale), and operating expense, and ranks products by *return per unit of bottleneck resource* rather than contribution — the throughput accounting ratio compares return per factory hour to cost per factory hour [T2] ACCA, "Throughput accounting", §"Step 2" and §"Ratios", https://www.accaglobal.com/an/en/student/exam-support-resources/fundamentals-exams-study-resources/f5/technical-articles/throughput-constraints2.html. In graph terms, each transformation node has a maximum throughput, the binding node is the global constraint, and capacity is a hard constraint attached to that node. Tax is best modelled as a jurisdiction-scoped, hard deduction attached to event nodes; its governance dimension appears even in sustainability rules, where EU Taxonomy minimum safeguards include required standards of tax governance, compliance, and behaviour [T2/T3] KPMG, "The first wave of CSRD reporting", §"The EU Taxonomy Regulation…", ¶2.

## 4. Legal and regulatory constraints as policy-as-code

Legal limits are the paradigmatic hard constraints, and the mature way to encode them is policy-as-code. The Open Policy Agent (OPA) decouples policy decision-making from policy enforcement so that "the people responsible for policy can read, write, analyze, version, distribute, and in general manage policy separate from the service itself", evaluating a high-level declarative language (Rego) over structured input [T2] OPA, "Philosophy", §"Policy decoupling" and §"Overview", https://openpolicyagent.org/docs/philosophy. The constraint-framework pattern makes each rule a `violation` set: a rule named `violation` returns matches with a message and optional `details` that support automated remediation, and a `ConstraintTemplate` declares the parameters a policy author may set [T2] OPA, "Constraint Framework" README, §"Rego Semantics for Constraints" and §"Rule Schema", https://github.com/open-policy-agent/frameworks/blob/master/constraint/README.md; [T2] Google Cloud, "Write a custom constraint template", §"Rego source code", https://docs.cloud.google.com/kubernetes-engine/policy-controller/docs/how-to/write-custom-constraint-templates. A regulatory rule attached to a node is therefore literally a violation predicate plus parameter schema plus evidence requirement — the same tuple as in §2.

Two contemporary bodies of law show how large and how parametric these hard constraints can be. The EU AI Act is risk-based: it prohibits a limited set of practices, imposes requirements on high-risk systems, and adds transparency obligations; high-risk systems must have a risk-management system, data governance, record-keeping, transparency to deployers, human oversight, and accuracy/robustness/cybersecurity [T2] European Commission, "AI Act" policy page, §"High risk" and §"Starting on 2 December 2027…", https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai; [T2] AI Act Service Desk, Article 9 "Risk management system", §1–5, https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-9. The Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) requires large and listed companies to disclose information on how sustainability matters affect the undertaking and how the undertaking affects people and the environment, according to European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS), with an assurance requirement [T2] European Commission, "Corporate sustainability reporting", ¶1–3, https://finance.ec.europa.eu/financial-markets/company-reporting-and-auditing/company-reporting/corporate-sustainability-reporting_en; [T2] EUR-Lex, Directive (EU) 2022/2464 (CSRD), recital 30. The EU Taxonomy supplies the alignment KPIs — turnover, CapEx, and OpEx — that make the disclosures checkable [T2] European Commission, "Omnibus" Staff Working Document, §2.3 "The EU Taxonomy", ¶2.

The crucial modelling feature is that these constraints are *conditional on size, sector, and jurisdiction*: applicability is triggered by thresholds (for CSRD, more than 1,000 employees and turnover/balance-sheet tests, with phase-in waves and an Omnibus simplification reducing scope) [T2] EUR-Lex, COM(2025) 81, §"The proposal introduces an opt-in regime…", ¶1. A growth optimiser must therefore re-evaluate which legal constraints are active as the business crosses thresholds; scope conditions belong in each constraint's `parameters`, not hard-coded.

## 5. Soft constraints: mission, values, brand, and ESG

Mission, values, and brand are not decoration; they are control systems that constrain and guide behaviour. Robert Simons' *Levers of Control* framework distinguishes belief systems (credos, mission and core values that provide direction and inspiration) from boundary systems (negatively phrased statements of what the organisation will *not* do, including business-conduct and strategic boundaries), alongside diagnostic and interactive controls; the power lies in how the four work together to create dynamic tension between innovation and predictable goal achievement [T2/T3] HBS Online, "Levers of Control", §"Belief Systems" and §"Boundary Systems", https://online.hbs.edu/blog/post/levers-of-control; [T2] valuebasedmanagement.net, "Levers of Control (Simons)", ¶1–2, https://www.valuebasedmanagement.net/methods_simons_levers.html. Empirical work confirms that firms emphasising the beliefs system also emphasise boundary, diagnostic, and interactive controls, and that the systems are complementary rather than substitutes [T1] Widener, "An empirical analysis of the levers of control framework", Accounting, Organizations and Society, §"Discussion", ¶1. In the constraint model, belief systems supply the *objective weights and tie-breaking order*, while boundary systems supply hard predicates of the form `action ∉ forbidden_set` — matching Simons' boundary systems as "a statement of what the company is not going to do" [T2] ICAEW, "Levers of Control", §"Boundary Systems", ¶1.

The hard problem is operationalising vague aspiration into checkable predicates. A working test is that an operating value does three jobs: it acts as a decision framework (a tiebreaker when two options collide), a performance expectation (what "good" looks like for a given role), and a behavioural contract (what is coached, rewarded, and no longer tolerated) — a value that cannot fill all three is a slogan [T3] Hagos Mehreteab, "The three jobs of a value", §"A value that's operating has exactly three jobs", https://talentforthewin.com/three-jobs-of-a-value/. Once a value is decomposed into observable behaviours, it becomes measurable: define the behaviour, choose a data source (survey, system log, HR record), set a threshold and frequency, assign an owner, and combine normalised indicators into a weighted score, `ValueScore = Σ wᵢxᵢ / Σ wᵢ` [T3] MIPA Overseas, "Values in Action Measurement", §"KPIs must be actionable…" and §"When you track several indicators…", https://mipaoverseas.com/values-in-action-measurement/. Behavioural records should be generated structurally — requiring every recognition event to carry a specific value tag, giver, recipient, and reason — and then tracked with five signals: coverage, spread, frequency/direction, depth, and correlation to business outcomes [T3] Jason Etter, "How to Measure Core Values", §"Step 4: Track the Five Signals", https://workproud.com/blog/how-to-measure-core-values-a-5-step-framework/. These map cleanly onto soft constraints: a value's composite score is a valued term in the objective, and its behavioural predicates are the per-node rules that generate the evidence.

Brand promise and reputation are enforced the same way, and the literature supplies a striking example of a value acting as a binding constraint: a WD-40 supply-chain worker rejected a cheaper ingredient worth roughly $4 million a year because it required a cancer warning label and the company's first value was "doing the right thing" [T3] Andrew Seerden, "Measuring Culture as an Operational Variable", §"Here is a concrete story…", https://seerdenboardpartners.com/measuring-culture/. The general mechanism for preventing a metric being gamed is to pair every optimised target metric with guardrail metrics: ticket deflection pairs with reopen rate and time-to-human-escalation, engagement pairs with regret signals (unsubscribes, session abandonment), and approval rate pairs with downstream default rate — designed by asking "if you paid a human to maximise this KPI, what would you regret?" [T3] Welzin, "Own the metric, not the model", §"Every target metric ships with guardrails…", https://welzin.ai/blogs/own-the-metric-not-the-model. Brand promise thus becomes a set of guardrail predicates bound to the same nodes as the metrics they protect.

ESG commitments are soft constraints with a schedule. Because CSRD requires undertakings to disclose "any sustainability targets set by the undertaking and the progress made towards achieving them", a public emissions or diversity commitment is a time-indexed valued constraint whose penalty rises as its deadline approaches; before the deadline it is soft, and missing it converts it into reputational (and increasingly disclosure) cost [T2] EUR-Lex, Directive (EU) 2022/2464 (CSRD), recital 30. Where values genuinely conflict with financial objectives, managers resolve the trade-off using emphasis frames and simple priorities such as "quality first, then service, then cost" [T3] Patricia Martyn & Breda Sweeney, "Ways managers can use company values to frame performance metrics", FM Magazine, §"Practical actions…", ¶3 and §"Cost over service?", https://www.fm-magazine.com/issues/2026/aug/ways-managers-can-use-company-values-to-frame-performance-metrics/ — which is exactly a lexicographic constraint ordering, a special case of a constraint hierarchy [T1] Barták 2002, §3, ¶1.

## 6. Runtime guardrails for agentic optimisation

When the optimiser is itself an AI agent, the constraint layer must be enforced outside the model, because prompt-level guardrails are only suggestions: a system prompt is an instruction, and so is malicious text the agent reads later, and the model has no reliable way to rank one above the other [T3] AgentShield, "AI Guardrails", §"Why can't I just put guardrails in the prompt?", https://agentshield.ai/features/ai-guardrails. Only enforcement in the action path holds when the agent is compromised, and three properties distinguish real enforcement: it runs outside the agent process, it fails closed, and it covers every hop rather than only the entry point [T3] AgentShield, §"Runtime enforcement means the check happens…", ¶1–3.

The research frontier has converged on a trigger/predicate/enforcement rule shape with formal guarantees. AgentSpec is a domain-specific language for runtime enforcement whose rules combine a triggering event, predicate conditions, and an enforcement mechanism (termination, user inspection, corrective invocation, self-reflection); it intercepts the agent's decision pipeline before execution and, in evaluation, prevented unsafe code executions in over 90% of cases with millisecond overhead [T1] Wang, Poskitt & Sun 2026, "AgentSpec: Customizable Runtime Enforcement for Safe and Reliable LLM Agents", ICSE 2026, §1, ¶3 and §"Contributions", ¶2. Agent-C targets *temporal* constraints — properties like "authenticate before accessing data" — translating specifications to first-order logic, checking each proposed tool call with an SMT solver, and using constrained generation with backtracking so that a non-compliant call is not merely detected but replaced by a compliant one, with guarantees rather than best-effort monitoring [T1] Kamath et al., "Enforcing Temporal Constraints for LLM Agents", §"Our Work", ¶1–2 and §"Constrained Generation".

Production policy engines generalise this into a decision core with a fixed verdict vocabulary. Microsoft's Agent Control Specification is a stateless, deterministic, *fail-closed* policy decision runtime: the host supplies a JSON snapshot at eight intervention points (`agent_startup`, `input`, `pre_model_call`, `post_model_call`, `pre_tool_call`, `post_tool_call`, `output`, `agent_shutdown`), and the runtime returns a verdict of `allow`, `deny`, `warn`, `escalate`, or `transform`, with optional evidence propagated to telemetry and runtime failures returning `deny` [T2] Microsoft, "agent-governance-toolkit / policy-engine", §"Runtime contract" and §"Verdict member", https://github.com/microsoft/agent-governance-toolkit/tree/main/policy-engine. The Agent Policy Specification wraps a model's `doGenerate`/`doStream` and evaluates input, each tool call, and output, throwing a `PolicyDenialError` that aborts the call on a deny [T2] Agent Policy Specification, "Vercel AI SDK Integration", §"withAps wraps…". A community protocol, the Agent Policy Layer, offers the same five verdicts plus composition strategies — `deny_overrides` (default), `allow_overrides`, `unanimous`, `first_applicable`, and `weighted` — and, like the others, fails closed when a policy server is unreachable [T3] Agent Policy Layer, README, §"Verdict" and §"Composition", https://github.com/nimonkaranurag/agentpolicylayer/blob/main/README.md.

This is the bridge between offline and online constraints. The `enforcement` field in the schema of §2 takes exactly these values: hard legal constraints use fail-closed `deny`; mission/brand guardrails use `warn`, `modify`, or `escalate`; informational limits use `observe`. Tool calls map to event nodes and tool results to their dual events, so a policy attached to a graph edge (e.g. "no payment event without an authenticated agent") is enforced by the same rule at the agent's `pre_tool_call`/`post_tool_call` points.

## 7. Surfacing violations and trade-offs to a strategy advisor

A constraint-aware advisor needs two distinct outputs: *feasibility repair* for hard constraints and *trade-off navigation* for soft ones. For hard constraints, the useful quantity is headroom expressed in time and value — the date a covenant is expected to be tested, the earliest forecast breach, the cash or operating movement needed to breach, and the action lead time — assembled into a covenant calendar including certificates, budgets, and draw notices, because a delayed certificate can create an avoidable default even when the business is within limits [T2] Matchpoint Partners, §"covenants", ¶"Headroom should be measured in time and value", ¶1. The advisor should compute headroom in both the base case and at least one downside scenario, and flag the point of minimum headroom (not merely current compliance) as the point warranting closest monitoring [T3] Oxxon Advisors, ¶"Headroom should be calculated at every scheduled test date…" and §"Common errors". For soft constraints, multi-objective optimisation returns a *set* — the Pareto front of solutions for which no other solution is better on one objective without being worse on another — and the decision-support task becomes helping the decision-maker explore that set rather than collapsing it prematurely [T1] Zatarain Salazar et al. 2023, "What Lies beyond the Pareto Front? A Survey on Decision-Support Methods for Multi-Objective Optimization", arXiv:2311.11288, §"Introduction", ¶2 and §"Conclusion"; [T3] Wikipedia, "Pareto front", ¶1, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareto_frontier.

Because preferences over mission, brand, and growth are "context-sensitive and are often calculated at the time a choice is to be made", a posteriori or interactive elicitation is more realistic than pre-committing to weights, and the advisor should therefore expose the trade-offs rather than present a single answer [T1] Zatarain Salazar et al. 2023, §"Introduction", ¶3. It should also report *recommendation stability*: modest perturbations of weights can cause rank reversals, so the advisor should quantify stability regions or sensitivity to weight ranges instead of overstating the reliability of a "preferred" plan [T1] MDPI Applied Sciences 2026, "From Pareto Front to Preferred Design: Human-in-the-Loop…", §"Uncertainty in the decision environment", ¶3. Finally, the theory-of-constraints focusing steps give the advisor a principled action order for hard capacity constraints — identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate, repeat — which converts a violation from a failure into an initiative [T3] Wikipedia, "Theory of constraints", §"The five focusing steps"; [T2] SAP, "Theory of Constraints", §"drum-buffer-rope".
