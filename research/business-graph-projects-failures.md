# Real Business-as-Graph Projects: Build Narratives and Failures

The vendor case for modelling a business as a connected graph — assets, events, agents, drivers, definitions — is easy to find. The first-person record is harder and less flattering. Across enterprise knowledge graphs, digital twins, driver-based financial models, and ERP "single source of truth" programs, the same pattern recurs: the graph gets built, the dashboards go green, and the project dies anyway. What follows is drawn from engineering blogs, conference-adjacent interviews, post-mortems, court filings, abandoned repositories, and peer-reviewed field studies, contrasting the survivors with the failures.

## The graph that never gets past the definition fight

The most common place a business graph dies is not the database. It is the meeting where stakeholders discover they have spent decades answering the same question four different ways. One practitioner account describes eleven weeks into an industrial knowledge-graph program and a standing meeting nobody scheduled: whether a subsidiary that buys through its own procurement entity on its own paper is "the same customer" as its parent. Sales, finance, legal and support each have a correct and incompatible answer. The graph cannot be built until one of them stops being correct, and "the routing-around move is also the standard way these programmes quietly fail" — you end up encoding the organisation's inability to decide, and every consuming system inherits the ambiguity [T3] studiox-ai, https://www.studiox-ai.com/prelogin/blog/what-is-an-ai-knowledge-graph.

A parallel account is blunter about the artifact that results. A client spent eighteen months building "the enterprise ontology" — a 2,300-entity model with 11,000 relationships, produced by four people, reviewed by nobody outside the team, and consumed by exactly zero systems. The diagnosis: three stakeholders used the word "ontology" to mean three different artifacts (a canonical data model for integration, a queryable knowledge graph, and a governed glossary of definitions), so one deliverable tried to be all three and shipped none. "The failure I see most often is a team that needed level one, was sold level three, and delivered level zero because level three never finished" [T3] Shirokoff, https://shirokoff.ca/blog/ontology-cdm-knowledge-graph.

The Data Praxis catalogue of enterprise KG failures formalises this as three patterns: the boil-the-ocean ontology, the no-consumer KG, and the governance-vacuum KG. Its archetype is a Fortune 500 program 21 months in with 200 million nodes and a billion edges, green dashboards, and no consumer — because the product team's roadmap moved on, the compliance team built its own SQL, and the sponsoring CTO rotated out. "The graph still works. Nobody is using it" [T3] The Data Praxis, https://thedatapraxis.com/blog/why-knowledge-graphs-fail/. The same source notes the arithmetic of sponsor churn: average CDO tenure is roughly 2.5 years against a 24-month build, so "every KG program at Fortune 500 scale will face at least one CDO transition during build-out" [T3] The Data Praxis, https://thedatapraxis.com/blog/politics-of-knowledge-graphs/.

Data-mesh implementations hit the identical wall from the decentralised direction. A six-month in-house data mesh produced three "customer" data products with three schemas, three ID formats, and three update frequencies; joining them for a dashboard was impossible, and "how many unique customers do we have?" returned three answers [T3] Reliable Data Engineering, https://medium.com/@reliabledataengineering/data-mesh-is-dead-and-thats-actually-good-news-building-decentralized-data-without-the-chaos-56910a13de45. NILUS, a Belgian consulting firm, describes "enterprise ontology decay" and warns that structural schema compatibility is necessary but not sufficient: a field called `customer_status` can pass every validation and still be meaningless without lifecycle rules and source-of-truth boundaries [T2] NILUS, "Data Mesh Without Contracts Becomes Data Swamp," https://www.nilus.be/blog/data_mesh_without_contracts_becomes_data_swamp/. Starburst's retrospective is terse: "The talent gap is structural, not temporary... You can't just tell a marketing team to 'own their data' when they've never managed infrastructure" [T2] Starburst, "Data Mesh: What Happened?", https://www.starburst.io/blog/data-mesh-what-happened/.

## Digital twins: component success, enterprise failure

The peer-reviewed digital-twin literature has begun to record a split between what twins do well and what they fail to do. Kober et al. document the rare economic success — a sawmill where a twin for the sorting system cut 'bin full' downtime from 5% to 2% of total downtime (2,411 minutes/year) despite a 25% throughput increase, over a four-year project. Notably, the technical deployment succeeded while end users rejected the twin's recommendations until team leaders enforced compliance: "human resistance to new technology can undermine even technically sound solutions" [T1] Kober et al. 2025, *International Journal of Production Research*, case study, ¶ on utilisation phase.

The failures cluster where the twin stops being a component model and starts claiming to be an enterprise model. A survey of 122 practitioners across process industries, infrastructure and manufacturing found that no sector was satisfied with delivered benefits, and that the split ran by role: Capex and IT professionals believed the twin delivered, while 73.3% of the Opex (operations and maintenance) group rated benefits as poor or not delivered, with one-third saying no twin was handed over at all [T1] Whitmore et al. 2024, *Proceedings of the ICE – Management, Procurement and Law*, §4.1–5.2. An interview study with 19 twin practitioners found "an overall lack of uniformity in terms of the understanding of digital twins and used tools, techniques, and methodologies," with model consistency, orchestration and validation repeatedly under-served [T1] Di Ruscio et al. 2024, *Software and Systems Modeling*, abstract and RQ1–3.

Case work shows the mechanism: twins get built as isolated asset models while "the enterprise anatomy linking engineering, maintenance, safety, and production remained unmodeled." An oil-and-gas review of the pattern has the chief enterprise architect saying the quiet part: "We have models of assets, not of the enterprise" [T3] ArchitectureRating, https://www.architecturerating.com/post/usa31-how-an-oil-gas-major-substituted-digital-twin-pilots-for-enterprise-architecture-design. A Dutch rail-station twin was launched by IT as a technical proof of concept with no business case; interviewees called it "a solution seeking a problem," and adoption failed because the stakeholders who owned the enriching data were never brought in [T1] Perša (ed.), *Study on Success Factors, Challenges*, University of Maribor Press, §4.5, challenges C4–C18. The cited failure statistics — 60–70% of sustainability twins never leave pilot, up to 75% missing ROI — usually trace to the data layer and governance rather than the simulation engine [T3] Sustainability Atlas, https://sustainableatlas.org/post/case-study-digital-twins-simulation-synthetic-data-a-pilot-that-failed-and-what-it-taught-us-1231.

## The three-statement and driver model as a graph that dies of maintenance

The financial-statement graph is the most honest test case, because it is small, fully specified, and has a hard correctness oracle: assets = liabilities + equity, ending cash ties to the cash-flow statement, retained earnings rolls forward [T2] ReWork, "Three-Statement Modeling That Survives Scrutiny," §checks. It still fails in predictable ways. Circularity (interest expense → debt → cash → net income → interest) is the classic break, usually resolved by lagging interest off beginning-of-period debt [T2] ModelMonkey, "3-Statement Modelling: Where Models Break," §circular reference. The deeper failure is organisational: the model is accurate in the base case and wrong under stress because working capital is hardcoded rather than tied to revenue [T2] ModelMonkey, §dynamic working capital.

Driver-based planning is the graph layer people actually try to operationalise, and it fails by losing its constraint. FP&A practitioners list the recurring failure modes: too many drivers built to please every stakeholder, offline Excel work that bypasses the model, scenarios that cannot be run because a changing driver is not linked to the model, and models that need specialist technical knowledge to alter [T2] FP&A Trends, "Driver-Based Forecasting in FP&A," §common pitfalls. The maintenance death is explicit elsewhere: "Comprehensive models require inputs nobody maintains, and the maintenance burden is what causes them to be abandoned" [T2] ChatFin, "How to Pick Drivers for Planning," §two failure patterns. Sarah Schlott names the brittleness directly: "Driver trees assume stability... The math isn't wrong. The assumption of constancy is" [T3] The Schlott Company, https://theschlottco.com/when-driver-trees-collapse-under-pressure/. Enterprise EPM platforms make this worse, not better: platforms implemented 8–15 years ago around consolidation keep driver-based capability dormant, and analysts revert to Excel because the platform workflow is slow or opaque — "the fourth phase is where most projects fail" [T2] DataQuant Consulting, "Driver-Based Budgeting," §workflow.

The survivors are specific and boring. HubSpot collapsed a 47-tab, 12,000-row workbook into 18 driver variables, and Q4 2024 revenue landed within 1.2% of the August forecast against a 6% historical variance [T3] MBA Training, https://www.mba-training.com/en/lessons/cfo-driver-based-forecasting. Atlassian reduced 30+ drivers to roughly 14 by demoting any variable whose ±20% flex moved EBIT less than 2% to an assumption. The rule that separates a driver from an assumption is ownership: "If finance owns the driver, it's an assumption, not a driver" — and driver creep back to 40 variables is the named failure [T3] MBA Training, same source. A €600M industrial distributor built eight drivers across an 18-week project, validating that the model reproduced 8–12 quarters of actuals before deployment [T2] DataQuant, §case study.

## The "single source of truth" that became a single point of failure

ERP is the largest real-world experiment in modelling a business as one connected record, and the public record is dominated by failure. On 4 April 2024 Lamb Weston disclosed that its North American SAP transition had "reduced the visibility of finished goods inventories located at distribution centers, which affected our ability to fill customer orders": a $135M net-sales hit, $72M net income, $95M adjusted EBITDA, and a $330M guidance cut in one quarter [T2] Lamb Weston press release, 4 Apr 2024, via FinancialContent, https://www.financialcontent.com/article/bizwire-2024-4-4-lamb-weston-reports-fiscal-third-quarter-2024-results-updates-fiscal-year-2024-outlook. The subsequent securities complaint records the market's verdict: shares fell 19.4% on disclosure and a further 28.2% in July 2024 when the recovery failed to materialise, with analysts citing "ongoing share losses stemming from the botched ERP transition" [T2] *Lamb Weston Holdings* class action complaint, D. Idaho, ¶¶6–9, 36–45. The stock move was the price of a graph that lost referential integrity: the company lost inventory visibility at exactly the node that connected warehouses to order fulfilment.

The pattern is not vendor-specific. Birmingham City Council's Oracle project went from an estimated £39M to an audited ~£90M and left the council without an adequate financial system for more than two years; Carpetright's failed replacement preceded bankruptcy [T3] lleverage.ai, https://www.lleverage.ai/company/blog/erp-implementation-failure-why-75-fail-at-189-over-budget-and-how-ai-automation-fills-the-gaps. A REIT spent $3.8M on a "successful" SAP implementation that was on budget and on time yet failing: three full-time staff reconciling modules, $2.3M in quarterly discrepancies, six-week closes, and a missed 1031 exchange deadline that cost a client $420K — because the system treated exchanges as extended transactions with extra fields rather than modeling the compliance choreography [T3] FintechZoom, https://fintechzoom.io/how-we-saved-a-reit-from-software-failure/. A consumer-goods company spent $1.2M and 18 months before anyone found the real problem: "400 customizations, all built to make S/4HANA behave like the old system they'd left behind. Every upgrade broke half of them" [T3] Ralph Hess, LinkedIn, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ralph-hess_by-the-time-they-called-us-theyd-spent-activity-7495859632804974593-tqCy.

The most instructive failure is not collapse but shadow re-creation. After a $10M+ SAP program, users still ran the business through Excel exports because the system of record answered the wrong question: "SAP is excellent at being a system of record... Users need a system of reasoning" [T3] Poorna Reddy, Medium, https://medium.com/@PoornaReddy/we-spent-10-million-on-sap-why-is-everyone-still-in-spreadsheets-31653aa54111. A CFO's own retrospective on a "plug-and-play" FP&A tool is the same story from the buying side: reports didn't match the ERP, KPIs were off, the logic was a black box, and after a year the tool was dropped [T3] Axile Talout, LinkedIn, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/axiletalout_fpanda-cfo-startegicfinance-activity-7388959255204372480-opLd. The Burgmann project, decades earlier, had already isolated the prerequisite: before launching ERP, the company spent six weeks defining shared recording rules and de-duplicating master data, because "a program installed on top of confusion does not reduce confusion — it gives confusion a faster engine" [T3] Gökhan Mercanoğlu, https://gokhanmercanoglu.com.tr/en/2002/01/05/erp-does-not-fix-broken-processes-burgmann-lessons/. A veteran CFO's account of ERP "scars" reduces the lesson to master data: "Good data hygiene at the master data and metadata level is the key input... it's 100x easier than trying to fix it once you are live," including one integration where legacy custom code forced a $500K outsourcing fix just to change a year-end date [T2] CFO Secrets, "System Rollout Scars: Finance Transformation III," https://www.cfosecrets.io/p/system-rollout-scars-finance-transformation-iii.

## REA and Valueflows: elegant ontologies, abandoned repositories

The Resource-Event-Agent (REA) ontology — McCarthy, 1982, standardised as ISO 15944-4 and recognised inside TOGAF — is the closest thing the field has to a principled business-transformation graph: every economic phenomenon is an event moving resources between agents, with duality linking give and take, and financial statements derivable rather than posted [T2] Wikipedia, "Resources, Events, Agents," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resources,_Events,_Agents. Valueflows extends REA for networks and ecological accounting, and its documentation claims the appealing property that "General Ledger, Balance Sheet, and Income Statement can be generated automatically from Valueflows data. No need to create a Chart of Accounts or post double-entries" [T2] Valueflows, "Accounting," https://www.valueflo.ws/concepts/accounting/. The theory is mature and shipped in academic tooling: an embedded DSL for recording REA transactions and extracting financials [T1] Itäniemi 2016, FAU thesis, §4.2; and an in-progress REA-native accounting system, REAcount, whose authors concede "this seems to be the first system implementing REA in this way [so] many things are uncharted land," with compliance, bank integration and CQRS still to build [T1] REAcount progress report, VMBO 2025, §"Iterative Process" and "future work".

The first-person record of production REA/Valueflows, however, is a graveyard with a maintenance problem. The flagship Holochain implementation, hREA, has an "effectively abandoned" GraphQL API; a March 2026 stabilization roadmap opens by noting the zome API is the de facto interface "without adequate documentation, stabilization, or alignment with ValueFlows v1.0" and solicits co-maintainers to "revive it" [T2] Nondominium/hREA, "strategic-roadmap.md," https://github.com/Sensorica/nondominium/blob/d20e1cfb/documentation/hREA/strategic-roadmap.md. A compliance audit scored the implementation at ~65% of VF 1.0, with five entity classes absent and "all entry validation functions are stubs with no business rule enforcement" [T2] Nondominium/hREA, "valueflows-1.0-compliance.md," https://github.com/Sensorica/nondominium/blob/d20e1cfb/documentation/hREA/valueflows-1.0-compliance.md. Several sibling repositories are explicitly archived or marked outdated: the metarepo warns "this project is highly outdated, and code should not be expected to run currently" [T2] h-REA/valueflows-project-metarepo, https://github.com/h-REA/valueflows-project-metarepo; the offers/needs marketplace carries the same warning [T2] h-REA/app-offers-needs-marketplace, https://github.com/h-REA/app-offers-needs-marketplace. The most active community path has migrated to ActivityPub via Bonfire, where the Valueflows extension lists Claim and Value Calculations as still "WIP" [T2] Bonfire, https://bonfirenetworks.org/extension/bonfire_valueflows/. A small AgTech implementation, GrowGood, reports the recurring friction that reads like an ontology-vs-practice tax: "Balancing the need for a concise, human-readable DSL with strict Valueflows ontological purity was an ongoing negotiation" [T3] GrowGood, https://growgood.org.au/en/blog/technical_journey/.

## What survivors shipped: instance graphs that improved decisions

Against that graveyard, a handful of organisations shipped maintained graphs whose absence would visibly break a consumer. LinkedIn is the clearest enterprise example at scale. Its Economic Graph powers real products on a graph database (LIquid) holding 270 billion edges and serving 2 million queries/second; migrating "People You May Know" onto it cut latency from over 1s to ~50ms and raised QPS from 120 to 18,000, and "developers can start an A/B experiment within hours by simply changing the query parameters" [T2] LinkedIn Engineering, "How LIquid Connects Everything," https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/graph-systems/how-liquid-connects-everything-so-our-members-can-do-anything. The graph is not a report; it is the inference substrate. Entity-BERT infers missing profile entities and, in online A/B tests, "audience expansion with Entity-BERT showed statistically significant impact on Ads revenue," while accepted skill suggestions drove engagement [T2] LinkedIn Engineering, "Completing a member knowledge graph with Graph Neural Networks," https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/knowledge/completing-a-member-knowledge-graph-with-graph-neural-networks. LinkedIn's Organisation Entity Resolution resolves external company records to graph nodes and exposes manual-override rates as a live model-quality metric — "the fewer overrides, the better" — a rare instance of a consumer-visible feedback loop on an enterprise graph [T2] LinkedIn Engineering, "Matching external companies to LinkedIn's Economic Graph at scale," https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/economic-graph/matching-external-companies-to-linkedin-s-economic-graph-at-scal. The LinkedIn case is what a maintained instance graph looks like: bounded use cases, named consuming products, and quality measured at the point of use.

Smaller survivors repeat the formula. A professional-services firm ingested a decade of documents into a graph and now answers ~200 queries/week for 40 professionals, turning 30–90 minute manual lookups into sub-10-second traversals and enabling questions "no one asked before because the manual effort would have been prohibitive" [T3] sftwtrs.ai, https://www.sftwtrs.ai/blogs/building-knowledge-graph-10000-page-document-library. A Fortune 500 deployment fixed cross-system entity resolution by starting with one division and one high-impact gap, then rebuilt trust in AI tools; crucially, "when someone corrects an AI answer, that correction feeds back into the knowledge graph" [T3] Phyvant, https://phyvant.com/blog/deploying-ai-knowledge-infrastructure-fortune-500. A developer-platform graph (cortex-product-graph) abandoned inferring structure from code and instead made JSDoc tags the single source of truth, enforced by ESLint and PR review, so "the graph is assembled from those" tags [T3] ryantsuji.dev, https://ryantsuji.dev/posts/cortex-product-graph. On the AI-memory side, a team that replaced a vector store with a temporal knowledge graph (FalkorDB + Graphiti) reports retrieval precision holding at 92% across 1,100+ sessions after vector retrieval "degraded to noise" by session 200 — and attributes it partly to storing far less, filtering to decisions and preferences [T3] Klement Gunndu, https://klementgunndu1.hashnode.dev/falkordb-graphiti-how-we-built-a-temporal-knowledge-graph-for-our-ai-co-founder.

## The decay clock: pilot purgatory, sponsor rotation, and the price of a judgment

Three mechanisms decide whether any of this lives past year two.

First, the funding model. Gartner's forecast that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by end-2027 cites "escalating costs, unclear business value or inadequate risk controls" — all project-management failures, not model failures [T2] Gartner press release, 25 Jun 2025, https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027. One analysis of the poll notes it rests on 3,412 self-selected webinar attendees and that 81% were conservative, watch-and-see, or absent — i.e., most spend sits in small pilots whose quiet death is the base case [T3] AI Enablement Academy, https://aienablement.academy/insights/why-will-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-get-canceled-by-2027. The Knowledge-Graph-as-graph applies the same logic: "A deliverable has an end state... A deliverable is funded once. A capability has an annual budget" [T3] The Data Praxis, https://thedatapraxis.com/blog/why-knowledge-graphs-fail/.

Second, the labour economics of semantic judgment. The LACE team's post-mortem is the sharpest statement of the real cost: "Every knowledge graph that ever worked at scale worked because somebody paid for human judgment, one data point at a time" — Google bought Freebase and paid curators, Wikidata recruited a volunteer crowd, defence systems bill analysts by the hour. Their claim is that LLMs only make this tractable when every remaining decision is reduced to a bounded classification, governed by a schema, an identity ledger, and an evidence chain [T2] LACE, "Knowledge Graphs Were Never a Technology Problem. They Were a Labor Problem," https://laceplatform.com/blog/knowledge-graphs-labor-problem/. A teardown of a personal AI-memory graph makes the same point from the opposite end: after three weeks, the system captured 2.4% of each document, built exactly one truncated knowledge item per document, and produced "zero graph edges" in a system whose entire purpose was relationships. "Technical success is not functional success. The plumbing worked, and the system failed at the only thing it existed to do" [T3] Lamco Development, https://lamco.ai/blog/knowledgepersistence-ai-teardown/.

Third, the persistence problem: a graph that nobody funds to maintain silently rots. Mozilla's Mentat, a persistent relational knowledge base "inspired by Datomic," was shipped and then marked UNMAINTAINED [T2] GitHub, mozilla/mentat, https://github.com/mozilla/mentat. An ontology-in-prose post warned that "an agreement with no consumer decays within two quarters; an agreement that breaks a build when violated stays alive" — which is why the surviving glossaries are those wired into a metric or a validation rule [T3] Shirokoff, https://shirokoff.ca/blog/ontology-cdm-knowledge-graph. Alation's framing is quoted approvingly: "the ontology you ship on day one is already drifting by day thirty," and "an ontology without a feedback loop is a snapshot" [T3] The Data Praxis, same source. Even a fully working commercial graph platform is not immune: Graphlit, a temporal-knowledge-graph-and-agent platform with paying customers since 2023, wound down in 2026 because it could not "turn that into a profitable, scalable business" [T3] Kirk Marple, LinkedIn, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kirkmarple_after-five-years-im-making-the-hard-call-activity-7479908273509294080-aBv9.

## What recurs across the successes and the failures

The evidence points to a small set of recurring rules.

- **A real consumer in the first 90 days beats a complete model.** The KG survivors ship a thin, ugly consumer application whose removal would visibly break someone; the failures "model forever and never ship," then lose their sponsor and budget [T3] The Data Praxis, https://thedatapraxis.com/blog/why-knowledge-graphs-fail/.
- **The definitions are the product; the graph is just how they are stored.** The resolution of "is a subsidiary the same customer" is a durable institutional decision with an owner, a date and a rationale that outlives the technology [T3] studiox-ai, https://www.studiox-ai.com/prelogin/blog/what-is-an-ai-knowledge-graph.
- **A named owner and a change process, enforced by tooling, prevent decay.** Ontology changes go through pull requests with deprecation windows so "the live ontology and the documented ontology cannot diverge silently" [T3] The Data Praxis, same source.
- **Start at the cheapest artifact that solves the actual problem.** Twenty agreed, enforced definitions beat a 2,300-entity model with none, and you learn within six weeks whether the organisation can agree on anything [T3] Shirokoff, https://shirokoff.ca/blog/ontology-cdm-knowledge-graph.
- **Master data and referential integrity are the graph's foundation, not its afterthought.** Lamb Weston lost $135M when inventory visibility broke; Burgmann and 400-customization ERP failures both trace to modelling the organisation's confusion rather than fixing it first [T2] Lamb Weston press release, https://www.financialcontent.com/article/bizwire-2024-4-4-lamb-weston-reports-fiscal-third-quarter-2024-results-updates-fiscal-year-2024-outlook; [T3] Gökhan Mercanoğlu, https://gokhanmercanoglu.com.tr/en/2002/01/05/erp-does-not-fix-broken-processes-burgmann-lessons/; [T3] Ralph Hess, LinkedIn, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ralph-hess_by-the-time-they-called-us-theyd-spent-activity-7495859632804974593-tqCy.
- **Unify infrastructure, distribute logic.** The data-mesh teams that recovered did so by centralising the platform and governance while keeping domain ownership of business logic, enforcing schema and quality automatically [T3] Reliable Data Engineering, https://medium.com/@reliabledataengineering/data-mesh-is-dead-and-thats-actually-good-news-building-decentralized-data-without-the-chaos-56910a13de45.

The uncomfortable conclusion for anyone building a business-as-machine harness is that the technology is rarely the binding constraint. Across four decades and four families of tooling — REA ontologies, digital twins, driver models, ERP — the failures are overwhelmingly definitional, organisational, and referential: the graph faithfully encoded an organisation that had not agreed on what its own words meant, or lost visibility of a node at the exact point where a decision depended on it. The survivors are not the most complete models. They are the narrowest ones that a named owner kept alive, whose definitions break a build when violated, and whose removal would be noticed by someone using it.
