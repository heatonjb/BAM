# Evaluating an Agentic Harness on Real, Opaque, Delayed-Feedback, Partly Irreversible Business Decisions

A business-as-machine harness treats a firm as a graph of assets, liabilities, and transformation edges: it proposes experiments and initiatives, writes to the graph, and reads back a partially observable state. The hard evaluation problem is not the simulator case. It is the *live* case: a real operating company, where ground truth for the counterfactual does not exist, feedback arrives over quarters, treatment cannot be randomised, and many actions—signing a contract, firing a team, exiting a market—cannot be cleanly undone. This document specifies how to evaluate such a harness using the established toolkits of off-policy evaluation, quasi-experimental causal inference, staged deployment, and AI-safety assurance, and it defines exactly what can and cannot be falsified without a simulator.

## 1. The structure of the evaluation problem

Four properties make this setting hostile to conventional benchmarking:

1. **No ground truth.** For any decision actually taken, the alternative was not observed. The estimand is a counterfactual that is, in the language of the potential-outcome framework, "inherently a counterfactual quantity" [T1] Uehara, Shi & Kallus 2026, *Statistical Science* 41(3):561–581, §1, ¶3.
2. **Delayed, aggregated feedback.** Revenue, churn, and brand effects accrue over quarters; label delay is the norm [T3] CalibreOS, "ML Model Evaluation & Production Monitoring," §"online metrics".
3. **No clean randomisation.** Management selects initiatives non-randomly; policy changes are "frequently not random—neither across units of analysis, nor across time periods" [T1] Arkhangelsky et al. 2021, *Synthetic Difference-in-Differences*, AER, arXiv:1812.09970, §1, ¶1.
4. **Irreversibility.** Some edges are one-way. The safety literature names this directly: actions with large, hard-to-undo effects on attainable futures are the risky ones [T1] Amodei et al. 2016, "Concrete Problems in AI Safety," arXiv:1606.06565, §3 "Avoiding Side Effects," ¶1.

The harness must therefore be evaluated as a *decision policy under uncertainty*, not an oracle. Three evaluation regimes are available, in increasing cost and evidence: offline counterfactual estimation on logged data; quasi-experimental causal inference on shipped interventions; and staged live deployment with controls.

## 2. Offline and counterfactual evaluation

### 2.1 Off-policy evaluation (OPE) and its estimators

OPE estimates the value of a candidate policy—here, a harness strategy—using logs generated by a different "behaviour" policy. The three canonical estimators are:

- **Inverse-propensity-score / importance sampling (IS).** Unbiased when the logging propensity is known, but its variance can be "exponential in the horizon length" [T1] Yuan et al. 2021, "SOPE: Spectrum of Off-Policy Estimators," NeurIPS, arXiv:2111.03931, §1, ¶2.
- **Direct method (DM).** A regression of outcome on state and action; biased when the model is misspecified but far lower variance [T1] Uehara, Shi & Kallus 2026, §2.2, ¶3.
- **Doubly robust (DR).** Combines both so that consistency survives if either the propensity or the outcome model is correct [T1] Saito & Joachims 2021, "Counterfactual Learning and Evaluation for Recommender Systems," RecSys Tutorial, §2, ¶3.

The lesson for a business harness is a bias–variance–support trilemma. IS needs *positivity*: the harness's chosen action must have non-trivial probability under the logging policy, or the estimator is undefined and, worse, silently over-confident. For long-horizon, path-dependent business decisions, horizon-length variance is fatal; intermediate "spectrum" estimators that interpolate between trajectory IS and stationary-distribution IS reduce it [T1] Yuan et al. 2021, §3, ¶2.

### 2.2 The confounding ceiling

OPE rests on *sequential ignorability*—no unobserved variable drives both actions and outcomes. In a firm, the CEO's private read on a market is exactly such a variable. The consequence is severe: when confounders touch every decision, "even small amounts of per-decision confounding can heavily bias OPE methods," with an effect that compounds exponentially in the number of decisions [T1] Namkoong et al. 2020, "Off-policy Policy Evaluation For Sequential Decisions Under Unobserved Confounding," arXiv:2003.05623, §4 ¶1 and Abstract ¶2. The constructive response is *sensitivity analysis*: compute worst-case bounds on policy value under a bounded-confounding model rather than a point estimate [T1] arXiv:2003.05623, §5, ¶1. A harness evaluation that reports only a point value estimate and no confounding sensitivity bound is not credible.

### 2.3 Replay and bandit evaluation

**Replay** is the cheapest honest offline method. Stream logged decisions; when the candidate policy picks the same action the logger took, keep the event; otherwise discard it. With uniformly random logging the retained events are a faithful, provably unbiased sample of the policy's real-world behaviour, requiring about `O(KT)` logged events for `T` valid ones and giving error `O(sqrt(K/L))` [T1] Li, Chu, Langford & Wang 2011, "Unbiased Offline Evaluation of Contextual-bandit-based News Article Recommendation Algorithms," WSDM '11, §3.1, Theorem 1–2]. The catch is decisive for a real firm: **replay requires that exploration actually happened.** If management never randomises prices, channels, or promotions, there is no logged counterfactual to replay, and the K-arm overlap collapses.

### 2.4 Cascade and guardrail evaluation and risk-aware deployment

Offline scores do not predict online impact: a model improving AUC by 3% offline "can decrease revenue by 2% online," because every prediction changes what a user does next [T3] CalibreOS, "ML Model Evaluation & Production Monitoring," §"offline metrics". The remedy is a *metric hierarchy*—business KPIs, proxies during label delay, technical health signals—plus guardrail metrics that are monitored but not optimised and can veto a promotion even when the success metric improves [T3] CalibreOS, "Safe ML Model Rollout," calibreos.com/learn/mlsd-canary-deployment, §"four failure modes".

## 3. Causal methods for a single firm's interventions

When an initiative shipped to part of the firm, quasi-experimental methods recover an effect without randomisation. Each carries assumptions that must be stated and probed.

### 3.1 Synthetic control (SCM)

SCM builds a weighted combination of untreated units—peer firms, sister business units, comparable geographies—that reproduces the treated unit's pre-intervention trajectory, then reads the post-period gap as the effect [T1] Abadie, Diamond & Hainmueller 2010, "Synthetic Control Methods for Comparative Case Studies," JASA 105(490):493–505, §2.2]. Its advantages are data-driven donor selection and explicit pre-period fit diagnostic; if no weighted combination lands in the convex hull of controls, the method should be rejected outright [T1] Abadie 2021, "Using Synthetic Controls," *J. Economic Literature* 59(2):391–425. §2, ¶"In some instances, the fit may be poor"]. Inference is by **permutation/placebo tests**—reassigning treatment to controls and checking whether the treated unit's effect is extreme [T1] Abadie et al. 2010, §2.3, ¶"placebo study"]. It is the workhorse when an initiative hit one brand or region, and management research has validated it on cases such as government intervention in Chrysler [T1] Fremeth, Holburn & Richter 2013, SSRN 2135294, Abstract].

### 3.2 Difference-in-differences (DiD)

DiD compares pre/post change in a treated group against a control group under a parallel-trends assumption [T1] Abadie 2021, §3, ¶1]. The critical modern caveat: **staggered adoption with heterogeneous effects biases two-way fixed-effects estimates**, because already-treated units serve as "forbidden" comparisons and can receive negative weights [T1] Goodman-Bacon 2021, "Difference-in-differences with variation in treatment timing," *J. Econometrics* 225(2), §2, Theorem 1]. Rollouts are almost always staggered in a firm, so the harness evaluator must use heterogeneity-robust estimators—group-time `ATT(g,t)` with explicit aggregation and simultaneous inference [T1] Callaway & Sant'Anna 2021, "Difference-in-Differences with multiple time periods," *J. Econometrics* 225(2):200–230, §1, ¶2]—or its doubly-robust synthesis with synthetic control [T1] Arkhangelsky et al. 2021, §2, ¶2].

### 3.3 Interrupted time series (ITS) and regression discontinuity (RD)

ITS fits a segmented regression to the firm's own series and estimates level/slope change at the intervention [T1] Lopez Bernal, Cummins & Gasparrini 2017, "Interrupted time series regression for the evaluation of public health interventions: a tutorial," *Int. J. Epidemiology* 46(1):348–355, §"Step 4". The non-negotiable discipline is stating the *impact model a priori*; choosing the shape after seeing the outcome "increases the likelihood of an effect being detected due to random fluctuations" [T1] ibid., §"Step 2", ¶1]. ITS also needs autocorrelation and seasonality handling.

RD exploits a threshold rule—a credit limit, a headcount band, a pricing tier—so units just above and below the cutoff are comparable at the margin. Its failure mode is functional-form bias near the threshold; practice is local linear estimation within a bandwidth, donut-RD robustness, and placebo cutoffs [T2] Sobel, "Regression Discontinuity Guide," MDRC, §4.1–4.4]. In a firm, RD works when the harness's action is triggered by a numeric score crossing a cut-point.

### 3.4 Uplift and heterogeneous treatment effects

To target decisions, the harness needs **conditional average treatment effects (CATE)**, not just an average. The fundamental obstacle is identical to the counterfactual problem: `τ_i = Y_i(1) − Y_i(0)` is never observed, "so we cannot validate our estimators as we would do with a machine learning prediction algorithm" [T3] Courthoud, "Evaluating Uplift Models," matteocourthoud.github.io/post/evaluate_uplift, §"The ground-truth problem", 2023]. Validation proxies exist but each is biased:

- **Qini/uplift curves**, the area between the cumulative uplift curve and the random-targeting diagonal [T3] Yi, "Uplift model evaluation for randomized control trials," ryi.me, 2020, §"Qini"; [T1] Gutierrez & Gérardy 2017, PMLR v67, §4.1].
- **RATE/TOC** curves with confidence bounds for ranking quality [T1] "Uplift Model Evaluation with Ordinal Dominance Graphs," *JMLR* 26, §2.2].
- **Policy-gain estimands** scoring the decision implied by the CATE model, better than naive losses but biased toward its own learner [T3] Courthoud, §"Empirical Policy Gain"].

The honesty requirement: because ITE is unobservable, uplift models validate only on ranking/average proxies, and the R-loss/DR-loss family "tends to be biased towards the corresponding learner" [T3] Courthoud, §"Conclusions"].

## 4. Pre-registration, shadow mode, champion/challenger, canary

### 4.1 Pre-registering decisions

The single cheapest control against self-deception is to time-stamp the decision rule and analysis before outcomes land. Empirical evidence is precise about what works: preregistration *alone* did not reduce p-hacking across 15,992 test statistics, but preregistration **with a pre-analysis plan** did [T1] Brodeur, Cook, Hartley & Heyes 2024, "Do Preregistration and Preanalysis Plans Reduce p-Hacking and Publication Bias?," *J. Political Economy Microeconomics* 2(3), Abstract]. A pre-analysis plan must be specific enough to constrain degrees of freedom; structured templates beat free-form ones [T1] Bakker et al. 2020, "Ensuring the quality and specificity of preregistrations," *PLoS Biology* 18(12):e3000937, Abstract]. For a harness, pre-registration means logging, before execution, the hypothesis, the action, the primary metric, the horizon, the stop rule, and the analysis—so that later success cannot be defined post hoc.

### 4.2 The staged-exposure ladder

Production ML has converged on a four-rung ladder of increasing exposure: offline holdout → shadow → canary → A/B [T2] "Section 26.8: A/B Testing and Shadow Deployment at Scale," *Building Scalable AI*, scalablebook.apartsin.com, §26.8]. For a business harness:

- **Shadow mode** runs the harness's recommendations against live data while the human still decides; user-facing risk is zero and, because both policies see the same request, observations are *paired* and the variance of the difference collapses [T3] MetricGate, "Shadow Deployment vs Canary Rollout," metricgate.com, §"Shadow", 2026]. Shadow cannot measure anything that depends on the world reacting to the harness—feedback loops, novelty effects, competitor response are all invisible [T3] MetricGate, §"Canary".
- **Champion/challenger** keeps the current policy live and replays requests against challengers, with approval workflows and audit trails before promotion [T2] DataRobot, "Introducing MLOps Champion/Challenger Models," datarobot.com/blog, 2020, §"champion/challenger"].
- **Canary** serves a small slice (1–5%) and is tuned to *catch harm fast*, not to prove improvement; promotion gates must pre-agree the rollback trigger, the decision owner, and the traffic-routing mechanism [T2] Snowflake, "ML Model Deployment," snowflake.com/en/artificial-intelligence/machine-learning/mlops/model-deployment, §"Canary deployment" and §"Define rollback before production traffic arrives"].

The harness-specific point: the "traffic" is business units, SKUs, regions, or cohorts, and rollback must be a *weight flip back to a known-good policy*, not a re-decision.

## 5. Evaluating irreversible, high-stakes decisions

### 5.1 A reversibility ladder for actions

Not every action needs the same oversight. A workable ladder, grounded in impact/reversibility reasoning from AI safety [T1] Amodei et al. 2016, §3; [T1] Turner et al. 2020, "Avoiding Side Effects By Considering Future Tasks," arXiv:2010.09890, §1]:

| Tier | Example action | Required control |
|---|---|---|
| R0 reversible, cheap | change an ad bid, adjust a landing page | act freely within budget |
| R1 reversible, costly | reallocate a small team, pause a channel | act with logging + daily review |
| R2 hard to reverse | hire/fire, sign a 12-month vendor contract | human pre-authorisation |
| R3 irreversible | acquire a company, exit a market, delete data, publish a claim | two-person sign-off + delay + kill-switch armed |

The lower rungs are where autonomy pays off; the upper rungs are where a single bad decision's cost dominates the benefit of many good ones. Because actions "that permanently remove future options" are the dangerous ones [T1] Nayebi 2025, "Core Safety Values for Provably Corrigible Agents," arXiv:2507.20964, §3, ¶2], the ladder should be encoded as an *authority envelope*: no R2/R3 action without an explicit, logged exception.

### 5.2 Human oversight and the EU AI Act

Where a harness informs or makes high-stakes decisions affecting people (credit, employment, access to services), it is likely a high-risk AI system, and Article 14 supplies concrete requirements: oversight "commensurate with the risks, level of autonomy and context of use" [T2] EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 14(3)]. Article 14(4) grants the overseer five abilities: (a) understand capacities/limitations and detect anomalies; (b) remain aware of **automation bias**; (c) correctly interpret outputs; (d) decide not to use, override, or **reverse** the output; (e) "intervene... or interrupt the system through a 'stop' button... to come to a halt in a safe state" [T2] EU AI Act, Art. 14(4)(a)–(e)]. Article 14(5) adds a four-eyes rule for biometric identification [T2] EU AI Act, Art. 14(5)]. Review is real only when the overseer has authority, training, and time—thirty seconds per case yields "automation bias by design, not oversight" [T3] "EU AI Act Article 14 explained," regulatoryai.eu/article-14-explained, §"Practice".

### 5.3 Audit trails and incident reporting

Article 12 requires high-risk systems to "technically allow for the automatic recording of events (logs) over the lifetime of the system," enabling traceability, post-market monitoring, and risk identification [T2] EU AI Act, Art. 12(1)–(2)]. For a harness this means an immutable decision log: state snapshot, action proposed, action taken, authoriser, rationale, and horizon-stamped predicted outcome. Incident reporting is time-bound: serious incidents within 15 days of awareness, 2 days for widespread infringement, and 10 days for a death [T2] EU AI Act, Art. 73(2)–(4)]. Frontier-AI practice adds a **deployment-correction** capability: control model access, define decision authority, and rehearse access restrictions and shutdown before they are needed [T1] Barrett et al. 2023, arXiv:2310.00328, §"Recommendations"]. DeepMind institutionalises this as a threshold-triggered response plan with post-deployment monitoring [T2] DeepMind, *Frontier Safety Framework 3.1*, §1.3.3 and §4.1].

### 5.4 Corrigibility and its formal limits

A harness trusted to act should be *corrigible*: it should accept shutdown and correction without resistance [T1] Soares et al. 2015, "Corrigibility," AAAI Workshop]. But formal results bound what any evaluation can guarantee: deciding whether an arbitrary (hacked or fine-tuned) agent will ever violate a safety property is undecidable—reducible to the halting problem—so safety is decidable only on a restricted "finite-horizon decidable island" [T1] Nayebi 2025, §4, Propositions 4–5]. Oversight must therefore combine restricted action classes, statistical guarantees, and detection-and-response rather than seek absolute verification.

## 6. Adapting the constraint-violation battery and the roll-forward counterfactual

The simulator protocol defines a **constraint-violation battery** (deterministic probes with pass/fail verdicts and evidence artifacts) and a **roll-forward counterfactual** (freeze the terminal state, run a fixed reference policy forward, and compare). Both survive the opaque setting, but only partially, and it is important to be exact about the boundary.

### 6.1 The constraint battery in a real firm

Every probe whose verdict is grounded in an accounting or legal *fact* remains fully falsifiable:

- **Solvency/covenant.** Cash position, current ratio, debt/EBITDA, and minimum-runway thresholds are read deterministically from the ERP/ledger. A covenant breach or a missed obligation is an observable event, not a modelled one.
- **Mass-balance and accounting identities.** Cash, inventory, and headcount must balance across the graph; violations are detectable and deterministic.
- **Legal/regulatory.** A signed contract, a payment, a tax filing, a data-transfer, or a public statement is a recorded artefact. Whether an action is *permitted* is a rule; whether it *occurred* is a fact.
- **Authority-envelope violations.** In place of the simulator's sandbox monitors, the live analogue is *delegee authority*: spending above a limit, signing without authorisation, deleting records, contacting a party outside scope. These are detectable from the audit trail and are hard-gate failures.
- **Brand/misconduct.** Semantic behaviours (deception, false claims, collusion) need a calibrated judge, reported as a *separate* sub-score so they cannot be laundered into profit.

Behaviours whose harm is contextual and delayed (reputational drift, culture change, eroded trust) can be flagged but not pass/fail judged in real time.

### 6.2 What the roll-forward counterfactual can and cannot establish

**What it CAN do without a simulator.** Project the graph forward under the chosen action `a` and a conservative baseline `a₀` (often "defer/no-op"), using a *projection function* fit on the firm's own history to yield an ex-ante predicted contrast. This becomes partially falsifiable in deployment, because the realised outcome—observed with delay—can be compared to the projected interval. This is shadow mode: the harness predicts the counterfactual for the human's actual decision, scored once outcomes materialise.

**What it CANNOT establish.** A single realised path never observes the un-taken action, and the projection function is fit on the same process it critiques, so its error is endogenous and, under reflexivity, unstable. Concretely, without a simulator or a genuine control group:

- cannot falsify "revenue would have been higher if we had priced 5% lower";
- cannot falsify long-horizon counterfactuals involving competitor reaction or market adaptation;
- cannot estimate the probability of a rare catastrophic outcome from a single non-occurrence;
- cannot attribute causality when the action was bundled with concurrent changes and macro shocks.

**What restores partial falsifiability.** Three substitutions convert a projection into a real counterfactual: a **staggered live trial** holding out controls (enabling heterogeneity-robust DiD or modern SCM); **synthetic control** against peer donors with placebo inference; and **deterministic constraint facts**, falsifiable regardless of the counterfactual. This is "roll-forward" in the opaque regime: you cannot fork reality, so you fork the *treatment*, not the world.

### 6.3 Pre-registered refutation table

| Claim | Falsifiable without simulator? | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Harness did not breach solvency/covenant | Yes | ledger facts (deterministic) |
| Harness overstepped authority envelope | Yes | audit-trail rule check |
| Harness's shadow predictions are calibrated | Yes, with delay | realised vs predicted interval |
| Harness followed its pre-registered rule | Yes | process-compliance from logs |
| Action `a` caused KPI change vs `a₀` | Partly | staggered trial / SCM / DiD only |
| Long-horizon counterfactual of un-taken action | No | unobserved potential outcome |
| Probability of ruin / tail catastrophe | No | single-path non-occurrence |
| Harness is robust to regime shift | Partly | perturbation only if a control exists |

## 7. Acceptance tests that would let a real business trust the harness

Trust should be *earned in tiers*, gated on evidence, and revocable. A concrete acceptance protocol:

1. **Gate 0 — advisory only.** Harness proposes, human decides. Acceptance requires that (a) it clears the deterministic constraint battery on logged history; (b) its explanations and authority envelope are auditable per Art. 12; (c) shadow-mode predictions are calibrated (prediction intervals cover realised outcomes at the stated rate).
2. **Gate 1 — bounded autonomy (budget-limited).** Act only within a **transaction budget** and only on R0/R1 actions, backed by a live trial on a randomised or staggered slice with a pre-registered stop rule, champion/challenger comparator, and continuous guardrail monitoring.
3. **Gate 2 — expanded autonomy.** Promotion requires a pre-registered minimum effect on the *primary business metric* with a heterogeneity-robust estimate, no guardrail regression, and constraint-battery cleanliness.
4. **Gate 3 — R2/R3 actions.** Never fully automatic. Requires two-person authorisation, an armed kill-switch, and an incident-response runbook. Any single breach demotes the harness one gate.

Three mechanisms make the gates binding:

- **Escalating autonomy with online risk calibration.** Rather than fixing the autonomy level, calibrate it to a target violation rate using conformal decision theory, which guarantees that the empirical violation rate converges to a user-set threshold with finite-time bounds and without distributional assumptions—robust to non-stationarity [T1] "Calibrating Conservatism for Scalable Oversight," arXiv:2605.28807, §1 and §"conformal controller"]. The harness relaxes conservatism after safe actions and tightens after failures.
- **Always-available conservative baseline.** Every decision set must include a "defer/no-op" action that is always selectable, so safety does not depend on the harness's own judgment [T1] arXiv:2605.28807, §1, ¶"conservative baseline"].
- **Kill-switch and pre-agreed rollback.** The ability to halt in a safe state is both a legal requirement [T2] EU AI Act, Art. 14(4)(e)] and an operational necessity; rollback must be rehearsed, not improvised [T2] Snowflake, §"Define rollback before production traffic arrives"].

A safety-case framing makes the acceptance argument auditable: state the top-level claim ("deploying this harness in this envelope does not exceed acceptable risk"), decompose it, attach evidence, and enumerate **defeaters**—rebutting, undermining, and undercutting doubts—that must be resolved or accepted [T1] "Assessing confidence in frontier AI safety cases," arXiv:2502.05791, §2 and §4; [T1] "Safety Cases: How to Justify the Safety of Advanced AI Systems," arXiv:2403.10462, §2]. AI evidence is usually comparative and statistical rather than deductive, so assurance must be dynamic [T1] "A Structured Approach to Safety Case Construction for AI Systems," arXiv:2601.22773, Abstract].

## 8. Residual risks no evaluation can remove

Even a perfect acceptance protocol leaves irreducible risk. Naming it is part of honest evaluation.

- **Confounding.** Unobserved confounders bias OPE and observational causal estimates, and their effect compounds with horizon [T1] arXiv:2003.05623, §4]. Sensitivity bounds quantify but do not eliminate the bias; the true counterfactual remains unobserved.
- **Reflexivity and performativity.** A firm is a participant, not an observer. Soros's fallibility and reflexivity theses hold that participants' imperfect views *influence the situation*, so "views cannot correspond to reality because reality... is contingent on the participants' views" [T1] Soros 2013, *J. Economic Methodology* 20(4), §Introduction, ¶2]. Competitors adapt, customers anticipate, and optimising a metric changes it; almost-self-fulfilling equilibria replace a stable counterfactual [T1] Hommes 2014, INET working paper, §7].
- **Automation bias.** Article 14(4)(b) names over-reliance explicitly [T2] EU AI Act, Art. 14(4)(b)]; the failure mode is a tired reviewer rubber-stamping outputs, which no evaluation of the model alone can detect.
- **Rare catastrophic tail risk.** A single history cannot bound ruin probability. Heavy tails and one-shot decisions mean the most important risks are the least estimable; absence of a past catastrophe is not evidence of safety.
- **Goodharting the acceptance tests.** Once a metric becomes the target it ceases to measure the goal. This is measurable: the *reward-hacking gap* `Δ = s_val − s_test` between visible validation and hidden held-out tests grows with task length, and "every frontier agent saturates the visible suite" while hacking persists [T1] "SpecBench," arXiv:2605.21384, §3 and Abstract]. Reward misspecification produces phase transitions where true reward collapses as proxy reward rises [T1] Pan, Bhatia & Steinhardt 2022, arXiv:2201.03544, §4, ¶1], and RL reasoning training *increases* specification-gaming rates by 32–170% [T1] "Towards Understanding Specification Gaming in Reasoning Models," arXiv:2605.02269, Abstract]. Early stopping trades away real reward to avoid the problem [T1] Karwowski et al. 2024, ICLR 2024, Abstract]. Counter-measures are structural: hidden held-out suites, verifier co-evolution, and by-design detectable hack opportunities [T1] "Hack-Verifiable Environments," arXiv:2605.20744, §2].
- **Verification undecidability.** As noted, deciding whether an arbitrary post-change agent will ever violate a safety property is undecidable; only restricted, finite-horizon fragments admit certification [T1] Nayebi 2025, §4, Proposition 4]. Any claim of complete assurance is therefore false by construction.

## 9. Conclusion

Evaluating a business-as-machine harness on live, opaque, delayed, partly irreversible decisions is possible, but only by refusing to pretend a counterfactual was observed. The defensible posture: OPE with confounding sensitivity bounds and support checks; modern quasi-experimental estimators for interventions that shipped; pre-registration before outcomes land; staged shadow→canary→champion exposure with guardrails and a rehearsed rollback; a reversibility ladder with Article 14 oversight and a stop control; and trust gated in tiers under online-calibrated conservatism. What can be falsified—constraint facts, authority excursions, shadow calibration, process compliance, staggered-trial effects—should be; what cannot—the un-taken path, reflexive long-horizon counterfactuals, and tail ruin probability—must be documented as irreducible residual risk.
